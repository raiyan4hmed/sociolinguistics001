The learning route

Chapters 1–6 establish society, identity, varieties, social variation, gender, and situational choice. Chapters 7–11 explain multilingualism, language contact, and change. Chapters 12–15 explain thought, communication, politeness, discourse, and power. Chapter 16 connects these ideas with World Englishes and teaching. Chapters 17–20 develop language policy and planning. The remaining chapters provide examination practice, short notes, revision, terminology, and references.

For each topic, read the example, explain what happens in your own words, attach the technical terms and scholar, then close the guide and answer the recall prompt. If you can repeat a definition but cannot explain the example, return to the explanation before moving forward.

One example connecting the course

Imagine Sakib, a university student from Jashore. At home he uses a local Bangla variety. With his grandmother he chooses respectful address forms. With friends he mixes English expressions into Bangla. In an examination he writes a standardized academic variety. At an international conference he uses English with participants who have different first languages. Later, as a teacher, he notices that a Chakma-speaking pupil struggles with a Bangla-only lesson but understands the subject in a familiar language.

Sakib's regional speech illustrates dialect. His friendship language expresses solidarity and identity. His respectful forms show politeness. His examination language illustrates style, register, and a standard norm. His conference English functions as a lingua franca. The pupil's difficulty raises questions about multilingual education, acquisition planning, and inequality. One person's repertoire therefore connects much of the course.

What sociolinguistics studies

Holmes and Wilson (2022, p. 1) state: “Sociolinguists study the relationship between language and society.” They explain that the field investigates why language differs across social contexts, what social functions it performs, and how it conveys social meaning. A sociolinguist asks why a student speaks differently to a friend and a teacher, why an accent attracts prestige, and how language choices relate to identity and social relationships.

Language is a structured system for making meaning through sounds, signs, words, and grammatical relationships. Society consists of people living within organized relationships and institutions. Culture includes socially learned knowledge, values, expectations, and practices. A real conversation commonly involves all three.

Example. A younger person asks an elder, “Apni kemon achhen?” but asks a close friend, “Tui kemon achhis?” Both ask about well-being. The difference also communicates relationship, respect, and intimacy. Neither pronoun has one universal social effect: “tui” may express affection in one relationship and disrespect in another. Grammar carries social information alongside the message.

How language and society influence each other

Social arrangements influence language because speakers learn which forms are expected in particular relationships. A hierarchical workplace may encourage titles and careful requests. Language also maintains or challenges those arrangements. If only one person can issue orders, interrupt, or define correct answers, their speech contributes to an unequal relationship.

Culture supplies knowledge needed to interpret language. “Come and have tea” might offer a drink, display hospitality, or open a social visit. The listener understands the likely meaning partly through familiar practices. Goodenough's account of culture emphasizes socially acquired knowledge needed to behave acceptably within a community. Such knowledge varies within societies as well as between them.

The influence can be reciprocal. A profession develops specialized vocabulary because its members do particular work. Learning and using that vocabulary can then help a newcomer participate in the profession. Social activity shapes language, while language helps organize participation in the activity. Wardhaugh and Fuller (2015, pp. 10–15) discuss different possible directions of influence between language and society.

Description prescription and the field boundaries

A descriptive account explains how people actually use language and the patterns behind that use. A prescriptive account recommends how they ought to speak or write. Sociolinguistics is primarily descriptive and explanatory, although its findings can inform educational recommendations. A teacher may prescribe Standard English for an essay while recognizing that a student's home dialect also has systematic grammar.

Micro sociolinguistics examines relatively small-scale relationships, such as pronunciation in an interview. Macro sociolinguistics examines wider arrangements, such as national policy and language maintenance. Sociology of language often emphasizes societal arrangements. The distinction is one of emphasis: a classroom language rule connects local interaction with national education policy.

Why the field matters

Sociolinguistics explains misunderstandings that grammar alone cannot resolve. It helps teachers distinguish a dialect difference from a learning difficulty and helps planners investigate whose needs a policy serves. It also questions the assumption that prestigious speech proves superior intelligence. A complete answer connects linguistic form with social context and its consequences.

Recall. Why do the two Bangla greetings have similar informational content but different social meanings? Identify a classroom decision that sociolinguistics can improve.

Speech community

Holmes and Wilson (2022, p. 690) define a speech community through a regionally or socially definable group whose members share a code and norms for its appropriate use. Wardhaugh and Fuller (2015, pp. 63–68) compare definitions, including Gumperz's emphasis on interaction and Labov's emphasis on shared evaluative norms. All English speakers worldwide do not necessarily share the same expectations about pronunciation, politeness, or turn-taking; a language name alone does not establish one speech community.

Example. Students and lecturers in a department may recognize respectful questioning, acceptable classroom humour, and appropriate examination style. They need not share one first language. Their expectations establish a relevant speech community, although they also belong to other communities. State which shared norms matter rather than assuming that every group automatically forms a single community.

Hymes uses these distinctions in the ethnography of communication. A situation can contain several events, and an event can contain many acts. A wedding includes greetings, negotiations, announcements, and speeches. A speech community is a group, not merely the place where conversation occurs.

Community of practice and social network

A community of practice forms through sustained participation in a shared activity, with developing practices and meanings. Wenger is associated with the broader concept; Eckert and McConnell-Ginet apply it in sociolinguistics. A debating club can develop characteristic expressions through regular joint work.

A social network consists of relationships among people. A dense network has many members who know one another. A multiplex tie connects the same two people in several roles, such as cousins who are also neighbours and coworkers. Milroy's Belfast research relates close networks to local speech maintenance. A network need not pursue one shared project.

Identity indexicality and stance

Social identity concerns how people understand themselves and are recognized by others through social groups and positions. Language can mark region, class, age, ethnicity, occupation, religion, gender, or nationality. These signals are interpretations, not infallible identity tests. A speaker may strengthen a regional accent among relatives, reduce some features in an interview, or challenge a listener's assumptions.

Analogy. A person has several socially meaningful roles, rather like several rooms in one house. Speaking differently in different rooms does not make the person fraudulent. It reflects the relationship and purpose that are active. The analogy has a limit: speakers can blend identities rather than move between separate compartments.

Indexicality means that a linguistic feature points toward a social meaning. A technical expression may index expertise without proving it. Stance is a position taken during interaction, such as confidence, doubt, sympathy, or distance. Recurring stances contribute to perceived identity.

Power solidarity and accommodation

Power is the ability to influence actions or control valued resources. Solidarity involves closeness and belonging. Brown and Gilman's work on address pronouns connects language with these dimensions. Bangla address forms can signal hierarchy, intimacy, or both, depending on context.

In Giles's accommodation framework, convergence makes speech more similar to an interlocutor's; divergence emphasizes difference. A student may slow down for a visitor. A regional speaker may retain local forms to express pride. Convergence can fail if exaggerated imitation sounds patronizing. Accommodation concerns social positioning as well as comprehension.

Recall. Identify a community, situation, event, and act in a viva. Explain how a pronunciation can signal identity without proving it.

Variety dialect accent and repertoire

A variety is a form of language associated with a group, place, situation, or function. The neutral term lets us discuss differences without first deciding whether they deserve the label language or dialect. A code is a language or variety used in communication. A linguistic repertoire is the collection of languages, varieties, and communicative resources available to someone.

A dialect can differ in vocabulary, grammar, and pronunciation. An accent concerns pronunciation. Two people may therefore use similar grammar and vocabulary but different accents. Standard English can be spoken with several accents; Received Pronunciation, or RP, is an accent historically associated with particular educated and privileged British groups.

Example. “I haven't got any money” and “I don't have any money” differ in grammatical wording. That can contribute to a dialect distinction. Two pronunciations of the same wording may differ mainly in accent. Dialect does not simply mean strong accent.

Language versus dialect

Mutual intelligibility is the ability of speakers of different varieties to understand one another without substantial prior learning. It is a useful starting criterion, but understanding can be unequal, exposure can improve it, and political institutions can recognize closely related varieties as distinct languages.

Asymmetric intelligibility means speakers of A understand B more easily than speakers of B understand A, possibly because of unequal exposure. A dialect continuum consists of relatively intelligible neighbouring varieties, with differences accumulating across distance. Far-apart varieties can become difficult to understand even without a sharp intermediate boundary.

Much ordinary Hindi and Urdu conversation is mutually intelligible, while writing systems, formal vocabulary, institutions, and identities differentiate the languages. Their separate recognition cannot be explained through intelligibility alone. Conversely, spoken varieties grouped under a broad label such as Chinese may differ substantially in intelligibility. These examples demonstrate the criterion's limits without dismissing speakers' own identifications.

Analogy. Intelligibility resembles the ease of walking between neighbouring houses. Political recognition resembles the administrative boundary dividing districts. Walking distance and administrative boundaries answer different questions, so they need not coincide.

Standard and nonstandard language

A standard has established norms supported by schools, publishing, administration, and dictionaries. A nonstandard variety differs from those norms. “Nonstandard” describes a social relationship to an institutional standard; it does not mean having no grammar or being unable to reason.

A teacher should distinguish an established dialect feature from an accidental slip. A standard can provide useful common conventions, while the claim that its speakers are inherently more intelligent is an ideology. Standardization is explained in Chapter 18.

Dialect geography

An isogloss maps a boundary for one linguistic feature, such as a pronunciation or word. Several overlapping isoglosses can identify a dialect area. One isogloss need not mark a language boundary. Dialectology studies dialect differences, traditionally with substantial attention to geography; sociolinguistic approaches also examine social variation within places.

Recall. Distinguish RP from Standard English. Give two reasons why mutual intelligibility cannot decide every language-versus-dialect case.

Variables and variants

Variation is the existence of alternative linguistic forms across speakers or situations. A linguistic variable is a feature occurring in alternative forms; those alternatives are its variants. Pronouncing the ending of “walking” with an ng sound or an n sound can be studied as variants of the variable conventionally written (ING).

The variable is not the social explanation. Pronunciation of r is a linguistic variable; class or speaking situation can help explain its distribution. Forms must be comparable enough in function or meaning for a particular analysis to be useful.

Labov and social stratification

William Labov investigated postvocalic r in New York department stores associated with different socioeconomic positions. He elicited “fourth floor” and then requested repetition. The distribution of r varied with the stores and with more careful repetition. The research connected pronunciation with stratification and attention to speech.

The finding is not that some customers are inherently better speakers. Language choices can reflect socially valued norms, and a speaker can alter a feature when the situation changes. Using stores as indicators of position is also a methodological decision rather than a complete measurement of individual class.

Overt prestige is openly recognized value, often attached to the standard. Covert prestige is positive value inside a group even when a form is publicly stigmatized. A local accent may be less valued in an interview but prized among friends as loyalty. Trudgill's Norwich work relates social patterns to these evaluations.

Class status and linguistic capital

Class relates to economic resources, occupation, education, and social relationships. Status is relative standing in a setting. A lecturer can have institutional authority without great wealth; class and status overlap without being identical.

Schools may reward language already familiar to privileged children, making a linguistic advantage appear to be an ability difference. Bourdieu's linguistic market explains how language acquires value in particular settings. Linguistic capital refers to valued linguistic resources that can bring social advantages. The same home dialect can have high value among relatives and low value in a recruitment interview.

Bernstein and the deficit problem

Basil Bernstein distinguished restricted and elaborated codes as communication patterns associated with social relationships and reliance on shared context. “Put it there; you know the place” relies on common knowledge. “Put the answer sheets on the second shelf beside the register” makes information explicit for a less informed listener.

These illustrate context dependence, not intelligence. Bernstein's account has been criticized when interpreted as showing that working-class speech is deficient. Labov challenges the idea that nonstandard speakers lack logic or expressive capacity. Distinguish unequal access to school-valued forms from an alleged absence of language ability.

Age ethnicity and education

Age affects language through peer relationships, life stages, and historical exposure. Age grading means a feature belongs to a life stage and may recur in later generations at that stage. Generational change means a newer cohort's usage contributes to continuing change. Youth slang alone does not establish permanent change.

Ethnicity can influence speech through shared history, networks, and identity. An ethnolect is associated with a community, but speakers vary and outsiders may acquire features. Language has no necessary one-to-one relationship with biological ancestry. Education can expand academic and standard-language resources, although some institutions also stigmatize home speech. Using both a local dialect and an academic register demonstrates repertoire rather than different levels of intelligence.

AAVE and systematic grammar

African American Vernacular English, or AAVE, refers to varieties associated with many African American communities; not every person uses every feature. Invariant habitual be, as in “She be working late,” can indicate a recurring activity. “She is working late” may instead describe an event occurring now. Treating the first as a failed attempt at the second loses the grammatical distinction.

Research methods

Labov's observer's paradox concerns studying natural speech when observation may alter it. Researchers compare tasks, interviews, and observations while interpreting their limits. Apparent-time research compares age groups at one time; real-time research investigates different historical times. Apparent-time differences may reflect age grading, so stronger evidence of change can require additional comparison.

Recall. Identify the variable, variants, and social factor in a study of r pronunciation across interview styles. Why can a context-dependent utterance be efficient rather than deficient?

Sex and gender in sociolinguistics

Holmes and Wilson (2022, p. 222) distinguish sex, referring to categories based on biological characteristics, from gender, which is used in sociolinguistics to discuss sociocultural behaviour, including speech. The chapter's question is how linguistic forms and ways of speaking relate to social expectations about women and men. It examines vocabulary, pronunciation, grammar, conversational functions, and the interpretation of those features in particular communities.

The distinction matters because a social pattern needs a social explanation. If women in a particular study use a standard pronunciation more frequently than men, a researcher must examine their social positions, activities, networks, and the meanings attached to that pronunciation. The result alone does not show that women have a different grammatical ability. It also does not establish that every woman in every society uses that form.

Gender exclusive and gender preferential features

Gender-exclusive features are conventionally used by one gender category in a community. Gender-preferential features are used by more than one category, but their frequency differs. Holmes and Wilson (2022, pp. 222–231) distinguish these patterns before examining explanations for them.

The book describes Yana forms in which certain words used between men were longer than corresponding forms used by women or when addressing women. This is a community-specific illustration of an exclusive contrast. By comparison, a pronunciation used by both women and men, but more often by one group, is a preferential feature. A frequency difference is like a difference in the popularity of a route to campus: more members of one group may take it, although the route is available to both groups.

Trudgill's Norwich research provides a useful connection with social class and prestige. Holmes and Wilson discuss how women and men within comparable social groups may use different proportions of standard and vernacular forms. The explanation involves the social value of the forms, including overt prestige and local solidarity, rather than a claim that one group has inherently better language. Class, occupation, age, and speaking situation must also be considered.

Deficit dominance difference and social construction

Wardhaugh and Fuller (2015, pp. 321–331) discuss several explanations of gendered speech. These are approaches to analysis, rather than four kinds of person.

In a deficit account, one group's speech is compared with another group's speech as the standard and is judged less adequate. Lakoff's discussion of women's language includes forms associated with uncertainty or deference, but her argument links these forms with women's social position. It should not be reduced to the claim that women are naturally poor speakers. Wardhaugh and Fuller (2015, pp. 323–324) explain this historical discussion and its limitations.

A dominance account asks whether linguistic behaviour reflects unequal power. In the interruption research discussed by the textbooks, the issue is who can take the floor, keep speaking, introduce topics, or prevent another person's contribution. An interruption can also express enthusiasm or agreement, so counting interruptions without identifying their function can be misleading. A chairperson's institutional authority may matter alongside gender. The analysis must connect the observed behaviour with the proposed explanation.

A difference account emphasizes different conversational expectations arising from socialization. Maltz and Borker discuss how the same response, such as “mhmm,” can be interpreted as listening or as agreement. Tannen's discussions contrast conversational orientations often called rapport and report. Misunderstanding becomes possible when a listener assigns a different function to the speaker's expression. Wardhaugh and Fuller (2015, pp. 325–327) also discuss criticisms: these accounts can make women and men appear more internally uniform than the evidence supports.

A social construction account asks how people use language to present themselves and how others interpret those choices. Holmes and Wilson (2022, pp. 446–449) discuss language as a resource for constructing gender identity. A person may use one style in a professional role and another with friends, because the activities have different expectations. The researcher examines those expectations and the interaction, rather than treating gender as a complete explanation before looking at the evidence.

Lakoff's features and the importance of function

Holmes and Wilson (2022, pp. 424–435) examine features discussed in Lakoff's account: hedges such as “perhaps,” tag questions, intensifiers, evaluative adjectives, precise colour terms, and especially polite forms. These features are useful for examination answers when their possible functions are explained.

Consider “The seminar starts at ten, doesn't it?” The speaker may be unsure and genuinely requesting confirmation. However, “That was an interesting presentation, wasn't it?” may invite another person to contribute. A teacher can also use a tag to encourage a quiet pupil. The grammatical form is similar, while the interactional purpose differs. Holmes's research distinguishes functions of tags and shows why they cannot all be counted as signs of uncertainty.

The same reasoning applies to hedges. “Perhaps we should check the figures” might express doubt, soften a disagreement, or introduce a careful suggestion. To decide, examine the preceding conversation, the participants' roles, tone, and response. Frequency and function are separate questions: knowing how often a form occurs does not yet tell us what it accomplishes.

Holmes and Wilson (2022, p. 453) state: “Sexist language encodes stereotyped attitudes to women and men.” Their discussion concerns how vocabulary and grammatical choices can express gender stereotypes. Wardhaugh and Fuller (2015, pp. 314–318) examine related asymmetries in labels, occupational terms, and generic references.

Gender neutral pronouns and the previous question

The 2021 paper asks about the benefits and challenges of gender-neutral pronouns. For that answer, begin with grammatical reference. Compare “If a student is absent, he should contact the teacher” with “If a student is absent, they should contact the teacher.” The second leaves the student's gender unspecified. A plural version, “Absent students should contact their teacher,” provides another comparison. Holmes and Wilson (2022, pp. 455–458) and Wardhaugh and Fuller (2015, pp. 314–316) discuss generic reference and linguistic alternatives.

The benefit to analyze is whether the wording conveys the intended reference clearly. Challenges can include familiarity, differing style conventions, and the fact that languages organize pronouns differently. Bangla third-person pronouns illustrate that gender distinctions and honorific distinctions are different grammatical matters. In an examination, explain the linguistic issue, give examples, and assess the alternatives in context.

Sexual identity in sociolinguistic analysis

The 2024 paper asks how language reflects and constructs gender and sexual identities. Wardhaugh and Fuller (2015, pp. 328–331) and Holmes and Wilson (2022, pp. 449–452) discuss this as an identity question: labels, styles, and conversational practices can acquire meanings through their use in particular groups. For example, a label's interpretation can depend on whether it is used by members of a group for themselves or by outsiders. The sociolinguistic task is to explain that relation between language, users, context, and social meaning. A pronunciation alone cannot establish a person's identity, and evidence from one group should not be generalized to every speaker.

Recall. Distinguish an exclusive feature from a preferential feature. Explain two functions of a tag question. Compare a dominance explanation with a difference explanation, and identify the evidence each would need.

The same meaning can require different wording

Imagine borrowing a pen. With a close friend you say, “Give me your pen for a minute.” With an unfamiliar lecturer you might say, “Could I borrow your pen for a moment, please?” The request is similar, but relationship and setting influence its form. Appropriateness means that an utterance fits the participants, purpose, setting, and relevant norms. A grammatically correct sentence can still be socially unsuitable.

Holmes and Wilson distinguish useful social dimensions: solidarity or distance, relative status, formality, and the balance between conveying information and expressing feeling. These are analytical dimensions, not fixed numerical measurements. A medical consultation may contain technical information and reassurance at the same time.

Register style and dialect

Holmes and Wilson (2022, pp. 370–381 and 687) discuss register as language associated with situations and, in a narrower use, specialist vocabulary associated with activities or groups. Wardhaugh and Fuller (2015, p. 53) connect registers with occupational and social activities. Style concerns choices within situations, including degrees of formality and orientation to an audience. Dialect is chiefly associated with the speaker's region or social group. Ask who speaks, what activity is taking place, and how the utterance is being presented.

Example. A doctor writes, “The patient presents with persistent dyspnoea.” To the patient she says, “You have been feeling short of breath.” Both concern medicine, but expertise and audience change the wording. The technical sentence belongs to professional medical usage; the accessible version adjusts style and language resources for patient understanding. Register does not mean that every sentence must contain technical jargon.

Holmes and Wilson (2022, pp. 10–13) organize language choice through participants, setting, topic, and function. They also distinguish social distance, status, formality, and the balance between referential and affective functions. The factors identify the situation, while the dimensions help explain the relationship between that situation and the language used.

Voice gesture and the surrounding situation

Holmes and Wilson (2022, p. 60) list pitch, volume, speech rate, facial expressions, and gestures among paralinguistic resources. These accompany linguistic wording and contribute to its interpretation. Saying “Fine” quickly in a cheerful voice can suggest agreement, while saying it slowly after a pause can suggest reluctance. The word is the same, but delivery supplies another cue. The example applies the book's account of multiple communicative resources; the cue must be interpreted in context.

Extralinguistic factors lie outside the linguistic forms themselves, including the physical setting, participants' relationships, and the activity. A teacher's “Come here” can be interpreted differently during a friendly discussion and a disciplinary meeting. Gesture, facial expression, and posture also contribute nonverbal information. These resources interact with language: a welcoming hand gesture can soften a brief invitation, while turning away can make the same wording seem dismissive. Gumperz's work on contextualization helps explain how participants use such cues to interpret an exchange.

Degrees of formality

Wardhaugh and Fuller (2015, p. 52) discuss variation from highly formal ceremonial language through ordinary formal occasions and informal conversation to conversation between intimates. They refer to Joos's discussion but emphasize the difficulty of drawing exact boundaries between levels. The main examination point is that speakers control a range of styles and choose among them in context.

Compare “Would you please take your seats?” at a formal event with “Come and sit here” among close friends. Both direct someone toward a seat, but wording, relationship, and occasion differ. An informal expression may be appropriate among friends and unsuitable in a ceremony. That does not mean it is grammatically defective. Likewise, a highly formal expression among friends may create distance or humour because it is unexpected in that setting.

Audience design and social judgement

Allan Bell's audience design connects style with who is listening, including an addressed listener and other audience members. A student answering a friend's question may change language when the lecturer enters. Giles's accommodation framework explains adjustments toward or away from an interlocutor. The speaker's purpose also matters: a comedian may deliberately imitate an institutional style to create humour.

Appropriateness is influenced by power. A senior person may be permitted to use a familiar address form that a junior person is discouraged from using. A critical answer therefore asks who establishes the norm and whether it imposes unequal burdens. Familiarity, directness, and politeness must be interpreted within the particular relationship.

Individual ability and societal organization

Holmes and Wilson (2022, p. 668) define bilingualism as “Knowledge of two languages.” The teaching discussion considers how these languages are used in particular relationships and domains. Multilingualism extends the account to the use of several languages. The textbooks do not require equal, perfect command of every language in every task: a person may speak one language confidently at home, read another academically, and use another in a limited workplace role.

Individual bilingualism concerns a person's repertoire. Societal bilingualism concerns the use and organization of languages across a community. A bilingual country need not contain only bilingual individuals. Fishman's distinction between bilingualism and diglossia further separates individual resources from the social distribution of language functions.

Example. A Bangla-speaking student reads English journal articles but rarely discusses family matters in English. Their abilities are domain-specific. Assessing only informal English conversation could underestimate their academic reading competence.

Domains and the choice of code

Holmes and Wilson (2022, p. 673) explain that a domain involves “typical interactions between typical participants in typical settings.” Their Chapter 2 discusses Fishman's use of domains to explain recurring language choices in activities such as family life, education, religion, and employment. A domain brings together the participants and activity; it is more than a physical location alone.

The choice depends on participants' competence, identity, relationship, topic, institutional rules, and practical access. In a hypothetical family, Chakma might be used with grandparents, Bangla in public administration, and English when reading international scholarship. This describes possible allocation, not a claim that every Chakma-speaking household follows the pattern. A bilingual person may also use several languages within one domain.

Competence and bilingual experience

The textbooks emphasize what people can do with their languages and where they use them. Equal skill in every language and every task is not a requirement. Wardhaugh and Fuller (2015, pp. 83–85) discuss multilingual competence, while Holmes and Wilson (2022, pp. 23–31) explain language choice through participants and domains.

Social advantages and unequal recognition

Multilingual resources may support communication, employment, family relationships, and cultural participation. However, benefits depend on the social value assigned to particular languages. English competence may be rewarded in a recruitment process, while competence in a minority language is overlooked. The difference is a feature of the linguistic market, not evidence that one person's multilingualism is intellectually worth more.

Some speakers face pressure to demonstrate loyalty by using a dominant language. Others can display several prestigious languages as cultural capital. This contrast helps explain elite bilingualism and immigrant bilingualism, the terms discussed by Wardhaugh and Fuller (2015, pp. 354–355): similar language resources may be evaluated differently because their speakers occupy different social positions. Avoid the universal claim that bilingualism automatically improves every cognitive ability or guarantees success.

Language choice in teaching

A teacher should ask which language resources help students understand, participate, and practise the target language. Brief use of a familiar language can clarify a concept; sustained opportunities for English use can develop fluency. A useful policy specifies the purpose of each rather than treating all first-language use as failure. Bilingualism does not automatically cause confusion, and code-switching does not itself show inadequate competence.

Ferguson's classical account

Holmes and Wilson (2022, pp. 32–36 and 672) explain classical diglossia as two distinct varieties in one speech community, with different social functions. Ferguson's account (1959) describes everyday vernacular varieties alongside a prestigious, superposed variety learned substantially through formal education. The labels H and L identify High and Low varieties in that arrangement. Their functions, acquisition, and social evaluation are the subjects of analysis.

Example. In a classical Arabic illustration, a prestigious formal variety is associated with formal writing or prepared religious discourse, while a local colloquial variety is used in ordinary family conversation. Present-day Arabic practices contain intermediate forms and considerable variation, so the classical model is a useful starting point rather than a complete description of every exchange.

Ferguson's four defining cases were Arabic, Swiss German, Greek, and Haitian Creole/French. These are historically situated cases. The Greek opposition he described should not be presented as an unchanged contemporary arrangement. Also avoid the claim that all his cases are simply two forms of one language: the Haitian case involves languages commonly distinguished as French and Haitian Creole.

Diglossia versus a formal conversation

Changing from casual to formal wording within English is not enough to establish diglossia. A diglossic analysis requires community-level allocation of varieties and relevant social history. Everyone being able to use two languages is also insufficient: bilingual ability concerns repertoire, while diglossia concerns social functions.

Analogy. A household may have two tools with distinct established jobs: one is used for daily cooking, another for a particular ceremonial preparation. Owning both tools is like bilingual ability; the socially expected division of their use is like diglossia. The analogy explains allocation, not language structure or prestige.

Fishman (1967) extends the concept to differentiated functions across distinct languages, including unrelated ones. A community might use one language in home life and another in administration. Under this broader approach, the language relationship matters less than the stable social allocation.

Sadhu and Cholito Bangla provide a historically useful comparison between a literary norm and a more colloquial base, but their distribution changed. 9 Lingua francas pidgins and creoles

Sections in this chapter

Lingua franca is a function

Holmes and Wilson (2022, pp. 119–120 and 680) define a lingua franca as a language used between people whose first languages differ. English may serve this function in an international discussion, while the book also discusses Swahili as a lingua franca in East Africa. The term identifies a communicative role; an established language, a pidgin, or a creole may perform it.

Example. A Bangla-speaking researcher and a Japanese-speaking researcher discuss their work in English. English is their shared medium. Its status as a lingua franca describes what it does in this exchange, rather than a special grammar that makes it a separate language.

Pidgin is a contact-language category

Holmes and Wilson (2022, p. 685) state: “A pidgin is a language which has no native speakers.” Their Chapter 4 explains its development for communication between people who do not share a language. Early pidgins often have restricted functions and reduced resources relative to the contributing languages. Wardhaugh and Fuller (2015, pp. 116–120) discuss definitions and their historical qualifications. Pidgins have patterns and conventions; they are not random errors or simply any learner's imperfect English.

A lexifier supplies much of the vocabulary. Superstrate and substrate refer to socially dominant and less dominant contributing languages in a contact history. These labels describe relationships and influences; a pidgin is not just the lexifier with incorrect grammar.

Historical trade, labour, colonialism, and enslavement created many contact situations. Not every such situation produces a pidgin, and not all contact-language histories follow one sequence. Vocabulary, sound patterns, grammar, and communicative conventions can draw on several contributing resources.

Creole and the traditional development account

In Holmes and Wilson's traditional definition (2022, p. 671), a creole is a pidgin that has acquired native speakers and expanded its grammar and vocabulary to serve first-language functions. Wardhaugh and Fuller (2015, pp. 116–122) discuss contact-language definitions and debates about origins. Learn the traditional pidgin-to-creole explanation and then qualify it: a clearly identifiable pidgin stage is not established for every creole history. Community use requires resources for narratives, explanations, relationships, and the wider range of everyday meanings.

Analogy. A temporary market communication system may become a community's language for the whole day and the whole range of life. The analogy helps explain functional expansion. It should not imply that adult pidgin users have no expressive capacities or that every creole must have passed through a clearly identifiable pidgin stage. Creole origins are debated.

Expanded pidgins can become stable and multifunctional without universal native acquisition. A creole need not possess an official standard to be fully systematic. These qualifications correct overly rigid claims that all pidgins lack tense markers or complex clauses and that all creoles are standardized.

Examples and terminology

Haitian Creole is a distinct language whose history includes substantial French lexical influence. Tok Pisin retains “Pidgin” in its name but has broad functions and first-language speakers as well as many additional-language users. Its name alone does not establish the acquisition history of every user. A mixed language, in specialist usage, is another contact-language category and should not be confused with occasional code-mixing.

Decreolization describes movement toward a socially dominant related standard under sustained contact. A creole continuum can contain a basilect relatively distant from that standard, an acrolect relatively close to it, and intermediate mesolects. These are relational terms. Not every creole community has the same continuum or follows inevitable decreolization.

The central examination distinction

Lingua franca identifies a communicative function. Pidgin and creole identify contact-language categories and histories. A pidgin or creole can function as a lingua franca, and an established language can also do so. “Trade language” therefore does not automatically mean pidgin.

Switching and mixing

Holmes and Wilson (2022, pp. 42–61 and 670) explain code-switching as moving between codes in a discourse or interaction. The classroom distinction often uses code-mixing for elements of different codes within an utterance. The textbooks also show that scholarly terminology overlaps, so state the distinction being used instead of assuming that every author uses the same boundary.

Example of an across-sentence switch. “Ami ekhon jabo. I will call you later.” The speaker moves from Bangla to English across a sentence boundary. Example commonly described as mixing. “Ajker assignment-ta submit korte hobe.” English-origin material occurs within a Bangla grammatical frame. The latter could also be analyzed as insertional switching or, if conventionalized, partly as borrowing.

Why speakers change code

Speakers may include someone, clarify a term, quote another person, express identity, signal solidarity, create distance, emphasize a point, or change topic. A university student may use the English technical label “validity” because it is the label used in the course, then explain its meaning in Bangla. The motivation need not be a missing Bangla concept or a desire to display superiority.

Gumperz investigates how switching acts as a contextualization cue: it can help listeners interpret how the next utterance relates to what is happening. Blom and Gumperz distinguish situational and metaphorical switching. Myers-Scotton's Markedness Model examines choices relative to expected social relationships. An unmarked choice fits a locally expected relationship; a marked choice draws attention to a different relationship or claim. These meanings depend on community norms.

Switching borrowing and language shift

Borrowing involves material from one language becoming established in another. A word can be borrowed even by people who cannot use the source language. Code-switching commonly involves activation of resources associated with different codes. Integration into local grammar and pronunciation, frequency, and community acceptance can help analysis, but single-word cases are often difficult to classify decisively.

Language shift is a community-level change in habitual language use and transmission over time. A person using English words between Bangla sentences has not thereby abandoned Bangla. Evidence of shift would concern declining Bangla use across domains or generations, such as children no longer acquiring or routinely using it. Switching can accompany maintenance as well as shift.

Translanguaging

Translanguaging emphasizes multilingual speakers' use of an integrated repertoire rather than assuming they always move between sharply separate mental systems. García and Li Wei are prominent in this approach. In teaching, a student might discuss a concept in Bangla, annotate English reading, and present an English explanation using the resources together.

Code-switching analysis often asks where socially named codes alternate; translanguaging asks how a speaker mobilizes resources to make meaning. These are analytical perspectives, not proof that named languages have no institutional reality. The perspective does not require unrestricted mixing in every examination or ignoring the need to learn target-language conventions.

Effects and classroom judgement

Code choices can improve comprehension, build solidarity, or signal expertise. They can also exclude listeners, reproduce prestige inequalities, or be judged negatively in some formal settings. Analyze the audience and outcome. A teacher can welcome familiar-language explanation while also requiring a later English performance for a specified learning purpose.

Change within a language versus change of language

Language change is alteration in linguistic forms, meanings, or patterns over time. Pronunciations, grammar, vocabulary, and usage can change while a community continues using the same language. Language shift concerns a community gradually replacing one habitual language with another across important domains or generations. These are connected processes, but they answer different questions.

Illustration. If a Bangla-speaking community begins using a new slang word, that is potential change within its repertoire. If successive generations stop acquiring their heritage language and use another language at home, that is evidence of shift. An isolated trendy expression cannot establish either a permanent change or a whole community's shift.

Social causes of language change

Contact, migration, urbanization, technology, education, prestige, networks, and identity can influence the spread of features. Internal linguistic patterns also matter. A prestigious feature may spread because speakers associate it with opportunity; a local feature may persist because it marks loyalty.

Labov's Martha's Vineyard research connects pronunciation with local identity and orientations toward the island. Change from above involves relatively conscious adoption associated with prestige; change from below develops below ordinary conscious awareness. These refer to awareness and social evaluation, not simply to upper-class and lower-class speakers. Lexical diffusion describes a change spreading through different words at different rates.

The same social conditions can produce different outcomes. Dense networks may maintain local norms, while contact through mobile speakers can spread innovations. A critical answer identifies the mechanism rather than claiming that modern society automatically destroys older forms.

A generational example

Imagine an immigrant family in Britain. Grandparents use Bangla for most home conversation. Parents use Bangla and English. Children receive most schooling and peer interaction in English. If adults also stop using Bangla with those children, transmission may weaken. The pattern is illustrative, not an inevitable three-generation law. Heritage schools, family interaction, media, and community networks can sustain both languages.

Shift can also occur without migration. A language can lose domains when schooling, employment, or administration increasingly requires another. Susan Gal's Oberwart study, discussed in the textbooks, relates Hungarian-to-German shifts to economic and social reorganization. Changing opportunities influenced language choice rather than German possessing inherent superiority.

Vitality and maintenance measures

Holmes and Wilson (2022, p. 93) explain ethnolinguistic vitality through three components: the language's status, the size and distribution of its users, and institutional support. A language with many geographically concentrated users, positive attitudes, and support in education and public activities has different maintenance conditions from a language with scattered users and little institutional support. The framework helps organize evidence; it does not make maintenance automatic.

The three components interact. A community can value its language but find few opportunities to teach or use it outside the home. A school can provide materials while family transmission continues to weaken. The planner should examine both opportunities and actual language use. Holmes and Wilson's chapter on maintenance and shift connects this analysis with demographic, economic, and attitudinal factors.

Maintenance measures should connect homes, schools, and community activities. Recordings and dictionaries preserve resources, but documentation alone does not establish daily use. Revitalization can include language nests, adult learning, accessible materials, and opportunities for ordinary conversation. Fishman's work on reversing language shift stresses the importance of intergenerational transmission; a ceremonial speech once a year cannot replace regular child-directed interaction.

The question behind the hypothesis

Does language merely express thought, or can the categories of a language influence habitual thinking? Linguistic relativity concerns possible effects of linguistic differences on attention, categorization, or other cognitive activity. Linguistic determinism is the stronger claim that language determines or confines what its speakers can think. They must be distinguished in an examination debate.

Analogy. Language can resemble a familiar route through a city. Taking that route repeatedly makes some landmarks easier to notice. It does not prove that other roads are inaccessible. This illustrates habitual influence; the claim that speakers are locked inside the route would illustrate strong determinism.

Edward Sapir and Benjamin Lee Whorf are associated with these questions, hence the conventional label Sapir–Whorf hypothesis. They did not jointly publish one neatly divided strong-and-weak hypothesis. The distinction is a teaching device used to organize later debate. Franz Boas's work also cautions against equating language, culture, and biological ancestry.

What evidence would establish

The availability of a word can make discussion easier, but not having a single word does not prove inability to grasp a concept. Bangla distinguishes kinship relations through terms such as “mama” and “chacha,” whereas English commonly uses “uncle” and adds explanation when necessary. An English speaker can still understand the maternal-versus-paternal distinction. This is an illustration of lexical organization, not experimental proof of determinism.

Some languages habitually use cardinal directions in spatial descriptions where English often uses left and right. Research discussed by Holmes and Wilson links these practices with attention to orientation. But speakers' environment, cultural training, and habits also matter. An appropriate claim concerns an observed tendency in specified tasks, not a universal inability to use other spatial concepts.

Colour studies investigate whether linguistic categories influence discrimination or memory. Findings about category-related effects support some kinds of relativity, while shared perceptual tendencies and the ability to learn new distinctions limit strong determinism. The existence of an effect in one task does not establish that language controls every aspect of perception.

Whorf Hopi and time

Whorf compared Hopi with what he called Standard Average European languages and argued that their grammatical patterns encouraged different understandings of events and time. This is historically important, but simplified claims that Hopi speakers have no concept of time are unreliable. Malotki's work challenged influential interpretations of Hopi. Explain the controversy rather than repeating the strongest claim as an established fact.

Language, experience, cultural practices, and cognition interact. A professional vocabulary helps doctors distinguish conditions, but professional training also creates the need for those distinctions. The causal relationship can run in several directions. Communicative competence

Communicative competence is the knowledge and ability needed to use and interpret language appropriately in social situations. Hymes argues that knowing grammar is insufficient. A learner also needs to know who may speak, when, to whom, with what forms, and for what purposes.

Example. “Give me your notes” is grammatically possible. Whether it is appropriate depends on relationship, tone, and situation. A student might use it comfortably with a close friend but choose a more considerate request when approaching an unfamiliar senior. Hymes's account asks about what is possible, feasible, appropriate, and actually done in a community.

Hymes and SPEAKING

The ethnography of communication studies communication as part of social and cultural life. Ethnography involves learning how members organize and interpret practices through observation, participation where appropriate, interviews, and attention to context. Hymes's SPEAKING framework provides a structured way to examine an event. It is not a method for measuring how loudly someone speaks.

Consider a student requesting an assignment extension during office hours.

Setting concerns physical time and place; scene concerns the culturally defined or psychological character of the occasion. The same office can host a serious consultation or an informal celebration. Ends include intended goals and outcomes, which may differ among participants. Act sequence concerns the content and organization of the speech. Key concerns tone or spirit. The framework explains why a communication failure can occur even with accurate grammar. A joking key during a serious request, or an unexpected act sequence, may change its interpretation. Categories inform one another; do not fill the table mechanically without explaining their effect.

Ethnomethodology

Ethnomethodology studies the everyday methods people use to make social activity understandable and orderly. Harold Garfinkel is its principal founding figure. “Ethno” here concerns members' ordinary knowledge; it does not mean that the approach studies only ethnic groups.

Example. At a counter you ask, “Two tickets to Dhaka, please.” The clerk does not normally ask whether “two” means two tickets or two buses. Both draw on the activity, the counter, and ordinary expectations about travel purchases. If the clerk asks “Which service?” you infer that an additional distinction is relevant.

Background knowledge includes facts and assumptions relevant to the situation. Commonsense knowledge consists of taken-for-granted expectations about how ordinary activity works. Practical reasoning is the process of using that knowledge to interpret what is happening and choose what to do. Participants achieve order rather than receiving a fully explicit script for every conversation.

Indexical expressions, such as “here,” “now,” and “that one,” require context. Ethnomethodological reflexivity means that an action responds to its context while helping establish what the context is. A teacher's “Let us begin” both fits a classroom and helps turn informal gathering into the lesson. Accountability means that actions are made recognizable and explainable to others; it is broader than blame.

Breaching and conversation analysis

Garfinkel's breaching exercises reveal background expectations by deliberately disrupting them. If someone treats a normal “How are you?” as a demand for a complete medical history, the mismatch exposes the greeting convention. In an examination, explain the principle through a hypothetical example rather than presenting disruption as good everyday conduct.

Conversation analysis, associated with Sacks, Schegloff, and Jefferson, develops detailed study of interactional order. Adjacency pairs link actions such as greeting and greeting, question and answer, or invitation and acceptance/refusal. Turn-taking concerns how speakers organize opportunities to speak. Repair handles problems in speaking, hearing, or understanding. Repair is not merely correcting grammar.

Comparing the approaches

Ethnography of communication asks how communication relates to the norms and practices of a community. Ethnomethodology asks how members make social activity intelligible through everyday reasoning. Conversation analysis examines the detailed organization of sequences. They can illuminate the same interaction from different angles. SPEAKING is Hymes's framework, not Garfinkel's acronym.

Utterances perform actions

A speech act is an action performed through language, such as promising, requesting, apologizing, warning, or declaring. Austin distinguishes the locutionary act, producing an utterance with content; the illocutionary act, the communicative action it performs; and the perlocutionary effect, what it brings about in a listener.

Example. A teacher says, “Please submit the script by noon.” The locution supplies the wording and content. The illocution is a request or directive. A student's submitting the script is a possible perlocutionary effect. The student might instead ignore it; the intended act and actual effect are distinguishable.

An indirect speech act expresses an action through a form associated with another action. “Could you close the window?” asks about ability grammatically but commonly functions as a request. Context and convention support that interpretation. A performative explicitly performs an action under suitable conditions, as in an authorized person's “I declare the meeting open.”

Wardhaugh and Fuller (2015, pp. 249–253) explain speech acts and the conditions under which they operate. Felicity conditions concern what must hold for an act to work appropriately: the speaker may need the relevant authority, and the situation must support the act. An authorized person's declaration can open a meeting, while the same words spoken during rehearsal have a different effect. Holmes and Wilson (2022, p. 390) also discuss promises and threats as commissives and examples of performatives or declarations.

Face and the two wants

Face concerns the socially recognized image a person seeks to maintain in interaction. Goffman's work underlies this discussion. In Brown and Levinson's model, positive face concerns being liked, accepted, or approved of. Negative face concerns freedom from unwanted interference and the ability to act without excessive imposition. Positive and negative do not mean good and bad personalities.

Example. “Your argument is foolish” threatens approval and competence, hence positive face. “You must lend me your laptop tonight” restricts choice, hence negative face. One utterance can threaten both. A respectful refusal may also protect the speaker's own autonomy while risking another person's approval.

Face threatening acts and strategies

A face threatening act, or FTA, creates a potential challenge to face. Requests, criticism, refusals, and disagreements can do so, but their effect depends on context. A medical warning may be direct because immediate safety outweighs the usual preference for elaborate mitigation.

Distance power and imposition

Brown and Levinson relate the weight of an FTA to social distance, the hearer's power relative to the speaker, and the culturally assessed ranking of imposition. Their often-cited formulation combines D, P, and R as analytical factors. It is not a calculator producing an objective politeness score.

Imposition is the burden a request places on another person's time, effort, resources, or freedom. Borrowing a pen for a minute may impose little; borrowing a laptop for a week may impose much more. The same request can also impose differently depending on the relationship and circumstances.

Implicature and cooperation

Grice's Cooperative Principle describes expectations supporting interpretation, not a moral rule that everyone must be agreeable. The maxims concern quantity, quality, relation, and manner. Implicature is meaning inferred beyond the explicit content. “The last bus has gone” can imply that the speaker cannot travel home by that bus.

Quantity concerns giving as much information as the exchange requires. Quality concerns saying what you have reason to believe is true. Relation concerns contributing something relevant. Manner concerns making the contribution clear and orderly. If someone asks where a classroom is, “On the second floor, beside the library” can satisfy all four: it gives useful directions, relies on the speaker's knowledge, answers the question, and uses clear wording. These expectations help explain why listeners search for another meaning when a contribution appears unexpectedly uninformative or irrelevant.

Flouting makes a departure conspicuous so a listener can infer an additional meaning. After a plainly disastrous presentation, “That went wonderfully” may invite an ironic reading. Violation conceals a departure and can mislead, as when someone falsely claims to have completed the work. Do not infer lying from every incomplete or indirect answer; knowledge and communicative intentions need evidence.

Cultural limits

Politeness conventions differ across relationships and communities. Directness can express warmth in one setting and sound abrupt in another. Brown and Levinson's universality claims have been debated, particularly regarding the role of social obligations and culturally specific understandings of face. A careful analysis examines the community's practices instead of treating the most indirect wording as universally most polite.

What discourse analysis adds

Discourse concerns language in use beyond isolated structures, including how texts and interactions organize meaning in social contexts. Discourse analysis examines that organization. Critical discourse analysis, or CDA, investigates how language participates in power, ideology, inequality, and their contestation. “Critical” means examining assumptions and consequences, not merely criticizing the speaker.

Ideology is a set of beliefs through which social arrangements are interpreted and often presented as normal. A language ideology concerns beliefs about languages and speakers, such as the assumption that a regional accent indicates low intelligence. Ideologies need not be openly announced or consciously deceptive; people can sincerely reproduce them.

One invented text and its social implications

Consider the hypothetical recruitment notice: “Only candidates with polished English should apply. We seek cultured people from good backgrounds.” The phrase “polished English” might refer to a real communicative requirement, but it needs definition. “Cultured” and “good backgrounds” can quietly substitute class judgements for demonstrable competence.

A descriptive analysis identifies evaluative wording, the restrictive “only,” and vague criteria. A critical analysis asks who can acquire the valued variety, whether the job actually requires it, and whom the conditions exclude. It does not assume that all English requirements are unfair. A translation position can reasonably require high language competence; a defensible criterion should connect competence with actual tasks.

How language reflects and exercises power

Power operates through control of speaking opportunities, legitimate labels, institutional genres, and access to valued codes. A court or examination board can decide what counts as an acceptable response. Speakers who understand the expected register have an advantage. When those conventions remain unexplained, the institution may mistake unequal familiarity for unequal ability.

Bourdieu explains the social value of language through linguistic markets and symbolic power. Myers-Scotton's elite closure concerns the use of language arrangements to maintain privileged access. For example, requiring a costly language qualification for an activity that does not need it may keep opportunities within an already advantaged group. The mechanism must be demonstrated in the particular case.

Fairclough and levels of explanation

Fairclough's three-dimensional account considers the text, the discursive practice through which it is produced and interpreted, and the social practice in which it participates. In the recruitment example, text includes the wording; discursive practice includes the employer's circulation and assessment procedures; social practice includes class inequalities and the value attached to English.

Text connection. Wardhaugh and Fuller (2015), pp. 291–302; Holmes and Wilson (2022), pp. 542–551.

Useful textual questions concern naming, pronouns, agency, modality, presupposition, and evaluation. “Workers were dismissed” may background who dismissed them, while “Management dismissed workers” makes the actor explicit. Passive grammar is not automatically ideological concealment: sometimes the agent is unknown or irrelevant. Explain the effect and test it against context.

Agency concerns who is represented as doing an action. Modality expresses degrees of certainty, obligation, or possibility: “must,” “may,” and “certainly” position a claim differently. Presupposition concerns information treated as already given; “When did you stop disrupting the class?” presupposes earlier disruption rather than neutrally asking whether it occurred. Evaluation assigns value through wording such as “responsible” or “unacceptable.” Analyze these meanings before drawing conclusions about ideology.

Discourse gender and identity

Holmes and Wilson (2022, pp. 446–449) discuss how ways of speaking acquire gender meanings in particular social contexts. An analysis can therefore examine who is represented as authoritative, what attributes are assigned to speakers, and how those representations are interpreted. The claims need evidence from the discourse and its setting; a researcher's expectation is not a substitute for analysis.

A sound answer distinguishes a textual finding from an inference. “The notice uses a male pronoun for every manager” is an observable finding. “It encourages a male-default image of leadership” is an interpretation that should be supported by the pattern and context. Van Dijk's work also connects discourse, social cognition, and ideology.

Analysis without mind reading

Begin with a small quotation or linguistic feature. Explain its immediate communicative effect, connect it with the institution or social relationship, and consider alternative readings. Avoid announcing the author's secret intention without evidence. Ideological effects can occur even without an explicit plan to discriminate.

Why the plural Englishes matters

World Englishes concerns the diverse varieties and uses of English shaped by different societies and histories. English has developed localized pronunciation, vocabulary, grammar, and pragmatic conventions. British and American varieties are also varieties; neither represents language without an accent or history.

A localized pattern is not automatically a learner error. Researchers examine whether it is systematic, shared, socially established, and useful within the community. A temporary individual mistake is different from a recognized local convention. Equally, calling every unusual form “local English” without evidence would remove an important distinction.

Critiques and complementary concepts

Countries contain considerable internal variation, and migration makes individual repertoires more complex than a national circle. Highly proficient speakers can be found outside the Inner Circle; first-language speakers do not automatically communicate more effectively in every international context. The model also gives limited attention to movement and changing digital communication. Use it as an influential map, not a permanent ranking.

English as a lingua franca, often abbreviated ELF, concerns English used as a shared medium by people with different first languages. Wardhaugh and Fuller (2015, pp. 115–116 and 388–391) discuss English's wide communicative role. World Englishes concerns varieties that have developed in different communities; lingua franca describes a function in communication. A locally established variety can also be used as a lingua franca.

The books explain how English develops local forms when it is used in new communities and institutions. Local vocabulary, pronunciation, and discourse conventions can reflect that social history. Compare a language's institutional role with the actual features of its varieties; a country's position in Kachru's model does not predict every individual speaker's language.

Teaching pronunciation and varieties

An EFL teacher should define the practical target. For an international presentation, intelligibility, appropriate stress, and listener awareness may matter more than imitating a socially prestigious accent. A model accent can be used consistently for teaching while learners hear several accents. Familiarity with varied English helps prevent treating unfamiliar pronunciation as automatically incorrect.

An academic-writing course may teach established grammar and genre conventions because those support readers' expectations. This can coexist with respect for local identity. The educational aim is to extend repertoire and communicative effectiveness, not erase a student's background.

Six practical classroom applications

  1. Teach requests through roles and relationships. Students compare asking a friend and asking an unfamiliar lecturer, then explain the difference rather than memorizing one polite formula.
  2. Teach register through real tasks. Students convert a technical paragraph into an explanation for younger learners and discuss what information must remain accurate.
  3. Distinguish dialect differences from learning errors. Avoid interpreting systematic home-language patterns as evidence of low ability.
  4. Use familiar languages for specified learning purposes, followed by meaningful target-language practice. Explain when and why each is useful.
  5. Include listening material with varied accents and intercultural encounters. Assess understanding rather than accent imitation alone.
  6. Make valued academic conventions explicit. Explain how a paragraph argues, how a source supports a claim, and how an examination response is organized.

Equity and language policy

If strong English is required for opportunity but only affluent learners can access effective teaching, English may reproduce inequality. With widely available, effective instruction, it can expand access. These possibilities connect sociolinguistics with acquisition planning. Respecting home languages and teaching English well are compatible goals when policies provide resources for both.

A school example before the terminology

Imagine a school that wants pupils to participate in English lessons without losing confidence in their home languages. It announces that pupils will receive regular English practice and may use familiar languages for specified learning tasks. That statement expresses a policy direction. Recruiting teachers, preparing materials, organizing bilingual support, and checking learning outcomes constitute planning and implementation. Whether pupils actually speak and learn as intended is a further empirical question.

Wardhaugh and Fuller (2015, p. 368), discussing Spolsky (2004), explain language policy through a community's language practices, language ideologies, and deliberate management efforts. Language planning concerns deliberate intervention in a language's form or function. Holmes and Wilson (2022, p. 678) define it as a “deliberate, systematic attempt to address the communication issues of a society” through study and policy concerning varieties' form, selection, and use. These accounts provide the foundation for the distinctions below.

Policy and planning often interact, but their relationship is not simply a rigid sequence in which a written policy always comes first. A community may develop teaching materials before official recognition. Everyday practices may also undermine a formally declared policy. Wardhaugh and Fuller therefore discuss the intertwined field of language policy and planning, commonly abbreviated LPP.

Spolsky and the three components

Spolsky distinguishes practices, beliefs or ideology, and management. Practices concern what people habitually do. Beliefs concern the values they attach to languages and appropriate use. Management concerns explicit attempts to influence practices or beliefs. These may conflict.

Example. A school handbook says all pupils' home languages deserve respect. Teachers believe English alone signals academic ability. Pupils are regularly punished for familiar-language explanation. The document, ideology, and practice are inconsistent. Analyzing only the handbook would miss the actual language conditions.

Explicit implicit de jure and de facto

An explicit policy states a rule openly. An implicit policy is inferred from recurring expectations and arrangements. De jure concerns legally established status; de facto concerns what operates in practice. These distinctions overlap without being identical. A private school's explicit English rule is not automatically a national law. A legally recognized language may have weak actual access in a service.

The absence of a written rule does not establish neutrality. Hiring staff who cannot communicate with a local linguistic community can effectively exclude that community even without an explicit prohibition.

The status/corpus distinction is associated with Kloss. Cooper gives acquisition planning a prominent place. Prestige planning is a useful additional dimension discussed in the literature, including the supplied Holmes and Wilson text; do not claim that every framework treats four categories identically. Some accounts discuss prestige within status planning.

Example of overlap. Recognizing a minority language in schooling is status planning. Developing its textbooks is corpus planning. Training teachers and creating learning opportunities is acquisition planning. Publicly challenging the belief that it is unsuitable for education is prestige-related work. One project can involve all these activities; classify the purpose of each action rather than attaching one label to the entire project.

Who plans and at what level

Planning can occur nationally, regionally, institutionally, or within families. Governments, academies, publishers, employers, teachers, activists, religious organizations, and parents can participate. Top-down planning is initiated by authorities; bottom-up planning arises through community activity. The directions can interact. A community literacy project may seek government support, and a national policy may require local adaptation.

Macro planning concerns broad societal arrangements; micro planning concerns more localized settings. These labels concern scale rather than importance. A family decision about which language to use with children can affect transmission, while a national declaration may have little effect without implementation.

Goals ideologies and competing interests

Planning may seek national unity, administrative access, literacy, economic opportunity, minority rights, revival, or international communication. Goals can conflict. Promoting one language may simplify some communication while disadvantaging others. Holmes and Wilson (2022, pp. 140–149) discuss the selection of national and official languages, while Wardhaugh and Fuller (2015, pp. 373–388) compare planning decisions in particular countries. Use these cases to explain the goals and the interests involved.

These are orientations, not four guaranteed successful policies. Ask whose definition of unity, efficiency, or opportunity prevails. “Language problem” can mean a real access obstacle, but it can also frame diversity as a defect before communities have been consulted.

Standardization and its purpose

Wardhaugh and Fuller (2015, pp. 33–37 and 418) explain standardization through the recognition of a variety as a norm and its codification. Holmes and Wilson (2022, pp. 149–166) develop the related processes of selection, codification, elaboration, and acceptance. Standardization therefore involves both linguistic resources and social recognition; it is broader than choosing a spelling rule.

Analogy. Agreeing on common road signs helps people navigate across regions. It does not prove that local ways of describing a route are inferior. Similarly, common language conventions can support communication while local varieties remain useful. The analogy also exposes the policy question: who decides which signs everyone must learn, and who pays for access?

Haugen and the four components

The four familiar components are selection, codification, elaboration, and acceptance. Haugen's work is central to their analysis; Holmes and Wilson present this account in their planning chapter. The components interact and need not occur once in a perfectly linear order.

Selection identifies the variety or norm to develop. Its existing reach, political support, associations, and acceptability matter. Codification develops conventions through spelling systems, grammars, dictionaries, and other reference resources. Elaboration develops resources for new functions, such as scientific writing or administration. Acceptance concerns whether institutions and speakers recognize and use the variety.

A Bangla illustration

The teacher's materials describe Standard Bangla's historical relationship with educated Nadia and Kolkata varieties. Use this as an illustration of selection from prestigious social and regional resources, rather than claiming a committee invented Bangla or that contemporary Bangladesh speech is identical to one locality. Dictionaries, spelling guidance, and grammars illustrate codification. Developing terminology and prose for education, science, administration, and digital communication illustrates elaboration. Adoption through schooling, publishing, and institutions illustrates acceptance.

Codification is one part of standardization. They should not be used as complete synonyms. Similarly, a dictionary can describe usage while being treated socially as an authority prescribing usage. A standard is an institutional norm that changes over time; it need not possess one compulsory accent.

Implementation and acceptance

Implementation means putting decisions into operation: preparing materials, training staff, arranging distribution, and supporting use. Acceptance concerns whether the selected variety is recognized and used by the relevant institutions and community. Holmes and Wilson's planning discussion (2022, pp. 149–167) shows why both practical provision and attitudes matter.

A dictionary can be published without teachers receiving copies, which is a problem of implementation. Teachers can receive it but continue using another spelling system, which raises a question of acceptance. In practice these problems interact: a difficult-to-use resource may weaken acceptance, while a well-supported program may encourage it. Use the distinction to explain what evidence a planner should collect.

Other useful frameworks

Cooper's focus on status, corpus, and acquisition widens planning beyond dictionaries and grammar. Spolsky's practices, beliefs, and management direct attention to the gap between formal rules and everyday life. Hornberger's integrative approach brings planning dimensions and approaches into a shared framework, helping show how form, function, and learning interact. Use these frameworks for the questions they answer; they are not interchangeable lists.

Hornberger's framework relates status, acquisition, and corpus planning to approaches commonly described as policy planning and cultivation planning. The policy approach emphasizes formal arrangements and norm development; cultivation emphasizes development of functions and use. For example, agreeing spelling conventions differs from encouraging writing in new genres, although both contribute to corpus development. Establishing a curriculum differs from expanding opportunities to learn and use the language. The framework helps a planner check both formal design and functional development rather than assume that producing rules completes the task.

Ricento's account of the field's development connects early postwar planning with decolonization and modernization, later critical approaches with inequality and access, and subsequent work with ideology, globalization, and linguistic rights. The stages are a history of changing research priorities, not a claim that every country passes through the same sequence.

Standard language functions

A common standard can help unite users and distinguish a community from others. It can symbolize identity, confer prestige, and provide a reference against which institutional conventions are judged. Garvin and Mathiot are associated with the unifying, separatist, prestige, and frame-of-reference functions. The classroom slides also emphasize identity. A frame of reference gives an accepted point of comparison; it does not turn every nonstandard form into an unintelligible mistake.

Benefits drawbacks and a balanced judgement

Benefits include shared publishing conventions, easier coordination, accessible reference materials, and wider learning resources. Risks include privileging the selected group's language, stigmatizing regional forms, and increasing barriers for learners whose home variety differs. Elaborating one standard can also overlook minority-language needs.

A fair response teaches access to valued conventions while respecting existing linguistic competence. Whether standardization supports inclusion depends on educational support, tolerance of variation, the functions assigned to the standard, and who participates in decisions. Standardization is therefore both a resource-development process and a distribution of social authority.

Writing systems and the development of resources

Wardhaugh and Fuller (2015, p. 406) define graphization as “the development and modification of writing systems.” Their Turkey case (pp. 373–375) illustrates orthographic reform through changes to the alphabet and associated language policy. Orthography concerns the writing conventions involved, including how forms are represented and spelled.

Holmes and Wilson (2022, pp. 149–156) discuss codification through grammars and dictionaries and elaboration through resources for new domains. Developing scientific terminology is an example of elaboration: speakers need expressions for concepts and procedures used in the new activity. Dictionary production can record conventions and make them accessible. These activities are related, but creating a writing system, describing grammar, and developing specialized vocabulary answer different needs.

National official and educational roles

A national language commonly symbolizes collective identity. An official language is authorized for specified governmental or institutional functions. A language may have both roles, and their meanings depend on the particular country and legal framework. A medium of instruction is the language through which subject content is taught. A language taught as a subject is not automatically the medium for mathematics or history.

Example. A school can teach English for an hour a day while teaching science in Bangla. The distinction between language-as-subject and language-as-medium matters because content access and target-language learning require different kinds of support.

Wardhaugh and Fuller (2015, pp. 377–378) describe how Belgium organizes language policy partly by territory. A geographically defined arrangement differs from giving all languages identical functions everywhere. The case shows why a policy answer needs to identify the community, institution, and area to which a rule applies.

Why familiar-language education matters

When a pupil already understands a concept but cannot express it in the school language, the assessment may confuse subject knowledge with language access. A familiar language can support participation and explanation while additional-language skills develop. This is a rationale, not a guarantee: teaching quality, materials, attendance, and assessment design also influence outcomes.

Planning cycle

Begin with a needs analysis: who uses which languages, what barriers exist, and what communities want. Consult affected groups, including those less likely to speak in formal meetings. Set explicit objectives, identify planning types, allocate staff and resources, pilot the design, implement it, evaluate outcomes, and revise. This is a practical synthesis of planning principles rather than a newly attributed single-scholar model.

Example of a measurable objective. Instead of “Promote the home language,” a pilot might aim to establish regular early-grade learning activities and materials in the locally agreed language, then assess comprehension, participation, and whether families use the materials. Set actual numerical targets after baseline data; this guide does not invent a survey result or a target that communities have never approved.

Evaluating outcomes fairly

Distinguish inputs, such as teachers and funding; outputs, such as materials distributed and lessons delivered; outcomes, such as improved participation and literacy; and longer-term impact, such as sustained transmission or wider access. Counting printed books cannot establish that pupils learned or that families maintained the language.

Combine observation, language-use surveys, interviews, assessment, and administrative evidence. Self-reported fluency can differ from task performance, and reporting may be influenced by prestige or fear. Compare outcomes across groups so an apparent average improvement does not hide increasing disadvantage for a minority. Without a suitable design, changes cannot automatically be attributed to the policy alone.

Planning goals and practical constraints

A policy can pursue unity, effective administration, language maintenance, educational access, and wider opportunity. Translation, teacher recruitment, terminology, and accessible materials cost money. Political disagreement and fear of reduced mobility can affect acceptance. An evaluation should identify how these constraints affect the particular program, rather than assume that all multilingual societies face exactly the same conditions.

Wardhaugh and Fuller (2015, pp. 370–372) discuss why researchers examine the social consequences and actual operation of policy. A school's stated aims may differ from classroom practices or assessment requirements. Study the mechanism: who understands the instructional language, what support is provided, and what an assessment actually measures.

Bangla and English need not be presented as an unavoidable choice between national identity and international opportunity. A more useful question is how access to both can be expanded and how minority-language resources are included.

Prestige also affects implementation. If pupils learn that their home language is embarrassing, formal recognition may produce little voluntary use. Respectful teaching, visible public resources, and community participation can counter stigma. Such measures should expand practical use rather than merely celebrate a language once a year.

Applying the textbook principles to a school

Suppose a school serves children whose strongest home language differs from the usual instructional language. Holmes and Wilson's discussion of maintenance and shift (2022, pp. 85–94) and Wardhaugh and Fuller's discussion of multilingual education (2015, pp. 351–355) provide the principles for analyzing this situation.

The school would first need to find out which languages children understand and use, what families want, and what teachers can teach. Materials alone would not settle the problem. Recognized classroom functions concern status, suitable resources concern corpus development, teacher preparation concerns acquisition, and community attitudes affect acceptance. The following proposal applies these principles to a hypothetical school; it is an examination exercise, rather than a report of a government program.

S1 Language can be understood through social life

Sociolinguistics studies the relationship between language and society. Language is a structured resource for meaning, but its use depends on relationships, purposes, institutions, and cultural expectations. The same sentence or form can therefore have different significance in different social settings.

Consider the Bangla choices “apni” and “tui.” Both can address a hearer, but they also express how the speaker understands a relationship. “Tui” may communicate closeness among friends and disrespect in another context. A grammatical description identifies the forms; a sociolinguistic explanation identifies their social meanings. Holmes and Wilson (2022) examine language choices through status, solidarity, formality, and communicative function.

Culture supplies learned knowledge for interpretation. An invitation to tea can initiate hospitality rather than merely offer a beverage. Shared expectations help participants recognize the action. Such expectations differ across relationships and communities, so neither literal wording nor a national stereotype is sufficient.

Language also participates in identity. Regional pronunciations, shared expressions, and multilingual choices can mark belonging. However, the relationship is not fixed: speakers select and interpret resources during interaction. A student may use a regional variety at home and academic English in a seminar while remaining the same person. Indexicality explains how a feature points toward a social identity without proving it.

The influence is reciprocal. Institutions attach value to standard varieties, while repeated use of those varieties can reinforce institutional authority. Language reform, such as adopting an inclusive professional title, can challenge an existing assumption, although vocabulary change alone does not eliminate inequality. Wardhaugh and Fuller (2015) connect language with identity, ideology, and power.

Sociolinguistics differs in emphasis from syntax or phonology because it asks how structures are distributed and socially interpreted. It also overlaps with pragmatics, sociology, and linguistic anthropology. At a wider scale, it investigates language policy and maintenance; at a local scale, it examines speaking styles and interaction.

Language must consequently be studied both as a structured system and as social action. Its forms convey information while helping establish relationships, identities, and access to institutions. Understanding society makes these meanings and consequences explainable.

S2 Language and dialect and the limits of mutual intelligibility

A dialect is a variety distinguished through vocabulary, grammar, and pronunciation. A language is commonly understood as a broader linguistic and social category, but the boundary is not determined by one purely structural test. Mutual intelligibility is relevant, while identity, institutions, and politics also shape classification.

Intelligibility asks whether speakers can understand one another without substantial prior learning. Closely related varieties may share enough resources for conversation, making the criterion useful. However, exposure changes understanding. If speakers regularly hear a prestigious neighbouring variety through education or broadcasting, they may understand it better than its speakers understand theirs. Intelligibility can therefore be asymmetric.

Dialect continua introduce another problem. Adjacent varieties may be intelligible while differences accumulate over distance. Speakers at opposite ends may struggle, although no single abrupt linguistic break divides all the intervening varieties. Political borders and linguistic transitions need not coincide.

Sociopolitical recognition further complicates the distinction. Hindi and Urdu share substantial resources in ordinary conversation, but writing systems, formal vocabulary, histories, and identities support their separate recognition. Conversely, spoken varieties grouped under a broad language label such as Chinese can show substantial differences in intelligibility. These examples demonstrate that a community's classification involves more than conversational ease.

It is also necessary to distinguish dialect from accent. Accent concerns pronunciation; dialect can involve vocabulary and grammar as well. Standard English is not identical to Received Pronunciation, because a standard variety can be spoken with different accents. Wardhaugh and Fuller (2015) explicitly discuss these relationships and the social role of standardization.

A standard dialect receives institutional support and codified norms. Other dialects nevertheless possess systematic grammar and can express complex meanings. Their lower status often reflects social hierarchies rather than linguistic inadequacy. Holmes and Wilson (2022) show how geographical and social variation interact with identity and evaluation.

Mutual intelligibility should thus be treated as evidence rather than an absolute definition. A convincing classification considers linguistic similarities, exposure, speakers' identifications, writing traditions, and institutional status. This approach explains both the structural relationships and the social consequences of naming a variety a language or a dialect.

S3 Social origins variation and identity

Language variation refers to alternative linguistic forms across speakers and situations. Its distribution can reflect region, class, ethnicity, age, gender, education, and social relationships. These factors influence speech through contact, opportunities, networks, and evaluation rather than mechanically determining every individual's language.

Regional dialects may differ in sounds, words, or grammar. Social dialects, or sociolects, are associated with social groups. Ethnolects relate to community histories and identities. These categories overlap: a pronunciation can carry regional and social meanings simultaneously. A feature indexes a possible affiliation but cannot infallibly identify a speaker's background.

Labov's department-store research illustrates systematic variation. The distribution of postvocalic r varied with the social positioning of stores and with more careful repetition of “fourth floor.” The study shows that socially evaluated forms are patterned and that speakers can adjust them. It does not establish inherent superiority among social classes.

Prestige helps explain such choices. Overt prestige attaches to openly valued institutional forms; covert prestige can attach to local forms that signal loyalty within a group. Milroy's network research further shows why close relationships can help maintain local norms. A speaker may therefore retain a regional feature for belonging while adopting another feature for a professional audience.

Age differences require careful interpretation. A form associated with adolescence may disappear as speakers age, producing age grading. Alternatively, a cohort may retain an innovation and contribute to ongoing change. Apparent-time differences need additional evidence before they are treated as permanent historical change.

Education can expand academic and standard-language resources, but schools may reward conventions already familiar to privileged children. Bernstein's codes concern communication and shared context; deficit interpretations become misleading when they turn context dependence into alleged intellectual inadequacy. Labov's work on nonstandard varieties challenges that assumption.

Language variation consequently expresses identity while reflecting unequal access to valued resources. A strong analysis explains how a social factor operates, illustrates the linguistic feature, and avoids treating groups as uniform. Sociolinguistics makes this patterned diversity understandable without confusing social prestige with linguistic capacity.

S4 Evaluating deficit dominance difference and gender construction

Language and gender concerns the relation between linguistic behaviour and sociocultural expectations about women and men. Holmes and Wilson (2022, p. 222) distinguish this use of gender from biological sex categories. Wardhaugh and Fuller (2015, pp. 321–331) discuss deficit, dominance, difference, and identity construction as approaches to explaining observed patterns.

A deficit approach evaluates a group's speech against a preferred norm and treats differences as inadequacies. Early discussions often used men's speech as the reference point. Lakoff's account drew attention to hedges, tags, intensifiers, and polite forms associated with women's language, linking them with social expectations and constrained authority. Its limitations include the need for stronger empirical evidence and the danger of assigning one function to every occurrence of a form.

For example, “The seminar starts at ten, doesn't it?” may request confirmation, whereas “That was a useful discussion, wasn't it?” may invite participation. Holmes and Wilson (2022, pp. 424–435) explain why tags must be analyzed by function. A tag is therefore insufficient evidence of uncertainty or weak confidence. The same applies to hedges and polite requests.

A dominance approach examines unequal conversational control. Zimmerman and West's interruption research is relevant because control of the floor can influence who is heard and whose topic continues. However, an analysis must distinguish an obstructive interruption from supportive overlap. Institutional rank, familiarity, and the activity may matter alongside gender. A numerical difference needs an explanation of its interactional meaning.

A difference approach connects conversational expectations with socialization. Maltz and Borker and Tannen discuss how participants may assign different functions to apparently similar expressions. One speaker's “mhmm” may mean “I am listening,” while another interprets it as agreement. This explains a possible route to misunderstanding. Its limitation is the tendency to present women and men as two uniform conversational cultures, despite differences within each group.

Social construction approaches examine how speakers use linguistic resources to develop and display identities in particular activities. West and Zimmerman discuss doing gender; Eckert and McConnell-Ginet emphasize participation in communities of practice. Holmes and Wilson (2022, pp. 446–449) show why a speaker's professional role, friendship group, and other identities can affect the relevance of gender in an interaction. The researcher must investigate the context instead of assuming that gender explains everything.

For the 2024 question on gender and sexual identities, add the textbooks' account of labels and styles acquiring social meanings within groups. A label used by members for themselves can have a different interpretation when used by outsiders. Explain the participants, setting, and meaning, and avoid treating an isolated linguistic feature as a reliable identity test. Relevant reading is Wardhaugh and Fuller (2015, pp. 328–331) and Holmes and Wilson (2022, pp. 449–452).

The approaches contribute different questions: what norm is used, who controls participation, what expectations differ, and how identity becomes recognizable. A strong evaluation compares their explanatory value against a specific example and recognizes the limits of generalizing from one community. It connects claims with linguistic evidence and social context.

S5 Language discourse ideology and unequal power

Language reflects power and also helps exercise it. Institutions regulate who can speak, which varieties count as legitimate, and what qualifications permit participation. Discourse analysis examines language in context; critical discourse analysis asks how its patterns relate to ideology and inequality.

An ideology is a set of beliefs through which social arrangements become meaningful and often appear natural. The assumption that polished English necessarily indicates intelligence is a language ideology. It overlooks how education, wealth, and exposure affect access to particular varieties. Bourdieu's linguistic market explains why language has different values in different institutional settings.

Consider a hypothetical advertisement requesting “cultured applicants from good backgrounds.” The wording does not specify a demonstrable job skill. Instead, it can turn class-associated resources into a criterion of legitimacy. A critical analysis identifies the evaluative labels, examines how applicants are assessed, and asks who is excluded. A genuine communicative requirement can be justified, but it should match the work involved.

Fairclough's framework relates text, discursive practice, and social practice. Text includes the notice's wording. Discursive practice includes its production and the recruitment procedures through which it is interpreted. Social practice includes class relations and the prestige of English. The framework connects a small linguistic feature with a wider institutional mechanism.

Gender identities can also be reproduced through discourse. Repeated descriptions of men as decision-makers and women through appearance can normalize different social roles. In interaction, unequal evaluation of similar assertive speech may reproduce gender expectations. A social-construction approach asks how such patterns make identities recognizable and how speakers challenge them.

Power also operates through access to languages. Myers-Scotton's elite closure describes linguistic arrangements through which privileged groups protect opportunities. Language policy can either reinforce those barriers or expand access through effective education and accessible public communication.

A critical answer must support interpretation with textual and contextual evidence. Passive grammar, indirectness, or English use is not automatically oppressive. The question is how a particular practice functions and with what consequences. Language and power are connected through social value, institutional authority, and the distribution of participation.

S6 Appropriateness and the ethnographic framework of speaking

Appropriate language fits participants, purposes, settings, and relevant social norms. Grammatical correctness alone does not ensure success. Hymes's communicative competence includes knowing how and when forms can be used in a community. His SPEAKING framework helps explain these contextual conditions systematically.

Consider a student asking for an assignment extension. Setting is the time and physical place, such as office hours. Scene is the culturally interpreted character of the encounter, here a serious consultation. The same room might host an informal celebration at another time, changing expectations.

Participants include the student and lecturer and their unequal institutional roles. Ends include the student's desire for additional time and the lecturer's concern for fairness and course requirements. Successful communication requires recognizing that the participants need not have identical goals.

Act sequence concerns how the exchange develops: greeting, explanation, request, response, and closing. Key concerns its tone, such as earnestness rather than humour. Instrumentalities concern the spoken or written channel and the selected language or variety. Norms concern turn-taking, appointments, and what evidence makes a request reasonable. Genre identifies the encounter as an institutional request or consultation.

These factors interact. A joking tone can change the interpretation of a serious request; a very direct command can conflict with the relationship; an email requires more explicit context than an immediate conversation. Holmes and Wilson’s social factors and dimensions and Holmes and Wilson's social dimensions provide complementary ways to explain the choices.

The speech community must be distinguished from the speech situation. The community is a group sharing relevant communication norms. The situation is a wider occasion, such as a viva. Within it, a question-and-answer event contains individual acts such as requesting clarification. These terms identify different analytical levels.

Norms also reflect power and vary across relationships and cultures. A form permitted to a senior speaker may be discouraged in a junior speaker. A strong answer therefore explains both the convention and who controls it. Appropriateness is contextual social knowledge, and SPEAKING reveals why the same wording can succeed in one setting and fail in another.

S7 Background knowledge and ethnomethodology

Ethnomethodology investigates the methods people use to make everyday social activity intelligible and orderly. Garfinkel emphasizes that social order is continually achieved through members' practical understandings. Communication works partly because participants share assumptions that are rarely stated explicitly.

At a ticket counter, “Two tickets to Dhaka, please” is understood as a purchase request. The clerk uses the location, ongoing activity, and ordinary knowledge about travel. Participants do not normally define “ticket,” explain what a counter is, or specify that two tickets are intended for travel rather than decoration.

Background knowledge includes facts relevant to the setting. Commonsense knowledge includes taken-for-granted expectations, such as a request receiving compliance, refusal, or a request for clarification. Practical reasoning is the process of selecting relevant knowledge to interpret an action and respond appropriately. If the clerk asks “Which service?” the customer infers that an additional travel distinction is needed.

Indexical expressions show the dependence on context. “That one” or “tomorrow” cannot be fully interpreted without the relevant situation. Reflexivity means actions both respond to and help establish context. A teacher's “Let us begin” makes sense in a classroom while helping transform a gathering into an organized lesson. Accountability concerns making actions recognizable and explainable to others, not only assigning blame.

Garfinkel's breaching exercises expose background expectations by disrupting them. Treating an ordinary greeting as a demand for a complete health report reveals the normally assumed function of the greeting. Participants often try to restore an ordinary interpretation rather than immediately abandon their expectations.

Conversation analysis develops detailed investigation of interactional sequences. A question projects an answer; repair handles problems in understanding. These are observable methods through which participants maintain intelligibility. Ethnography of communication instead gives particular attention to community norms and activities, with Hymes's SPEAKING framework as a useful guide.

Ethnomethodology therefore shows that meaning is an interactional achievement. Grammar supplies resources, while background knowledge and practical reasoning help people recognize what is happening. An answer should demonstrate this through a complete ordinary exchange rather than merely list the technical terms.

S8 A balanced debate on linguistic determinism

Linguistic determinism is the strong claim that language determines or confines thought. Linguistic relativity is a more qualified account of how linguistic categories may influence habitual attention and cognition. The conventional Sapir–Whorf label covers important questions about their relationship, but the strong and weak versions should be distinguished.

Different languages organize meanings differently. Bangla kinship terms can distinguish relations represented by the broader English word “uncle.” These terms make a distinction readily available in routine expression. However, English speakers can understand the relationship through explanation. Lexical difference therefore does not prove conceptual impossibility.

Grammatical requirements can direct attention toward distinctions that must be expressed. Spatial systems based on cardinal directions and research on colour categories suggest that routine language practices can be associated with differences in particular tasks. Holmes and Wilson (2022) discuss these findings in relation to culture and cognition. The evidence supports specified influences rather than language controlling every aspect of perception.

Whorf's discussion of Hopi and Standard Average European languages is historically influential. Simplified claims that Hopi speakers lack time concepts are not reliable conclusions. Malotki's work challenges important interpretations. The example is valuable as a debate about linguistic analysis and evidence, not as proof that speakers inhabit incomparable realities.

Several arguments limit strong determinism. People translate ideas, develop new vocabulary, learn other languages, and reason nonverbally. A person can understand a concept before knowing a conventional label for it. Pinker emphasizes the distinction between language and thought. These observations challenge the idea of an absolute linguistic prison.

However, rejecting determinism does not establish that language has no influence. Familiar categories and obligatory distinctions can affect what people notice or retrieve quickly. Language, culture, environment, and experience interact, making causal claims difficult. Research must specify the linguistic feature, cognitive task, controls, and limits of generalization.

The strongest conclusion therefore rejects unsupported absolute determinism while allowing evidence for limited relativity. A balanced debate explains both why language can influence habitual thinking and why speakers can think beyond the resources immediately available in their first language.

S9 Standardization its process benefits and drawbacks

Standardization is the process through which a variety develops relatively established norms and recognized institutional functions. It commonly involves selection, codification, elaboration, and acceptance. Haugen's account, presented in Holmes and Wilson (2022), connects social decisions about a variety with linguistic development.

Selection identifies the variety or norm to develop. This is influenced by reach, political power, identity, and acceptability rather than inherent linguistic superiority. The teacher's Bangla materials illustrate the historical influence of educated Nadia and Kolkata varieties. Their prestige helped establish a basis for standard norms; this does not mean all Bangla speakers naturally use identical speech.

Codification establishes reference conventions through spelling guidance, grammars, dictionaries, and related resources. It reduces uncertainty about institutional usage. Codification is one component of standardization, not the entire process. Reference works can also change as usage develops.

Elaboration expands resources for new functions. A variety used in ordinary interaction may need terminology and genres for science, law, administration, or digital communication. Developing these resources shows that languages can adapt to new purposes; their previous lack of specialized terminology does not establish intellectual inferiority.

Acceptance concerns recognition and use by institutions and speakers. School adoption or publication can encourage it, but distribution does not guarantee willingness or effective use. Haugen's later implementation formulation highlights the practical work of spreading and supporting a norm. Implementation and acceptance should be related without being treated as identical.

Standardization provides shared conventions, facilitates publishing and administration, and supports common educational resources. It may also symbolize collective identity. Garvin and Mathiot discuss unifying, separatist, prestige, and frame-of-reference functions. The standard helps coordinate communication while marking social membership and authority.

However, selecting one variety can disadvantage others. Schools may stigmatize home dialects or interpret unfamiliarity with the standard as low ability. Wardhaugh and Fuller (2015) show how the standard–dialect hierarchy reflects power. Nonstandard varieties nevertheless have systematic grammar and expressive capacity.

A balanced evaluation therefore supports access to useful common conventions while resisting unjustified deficit judgements. Standardization can widen access when education explains its norms and resources reach learners whose home varieties differ. Its value depends on how it is developed and used, rather than its mere existence.

S10 Code switching and mixing reasons and effects

Code-switching is alternation between languages or varieties in interaction. In a common classroom distinction, code-mixing refers to incorporating elements from different codes inside an utterance. Scholarly terminology overlaps, so the distinction must be stated rather than treated as universal.

“Ami ekhon jabo. I will call you later” illustrates switching across sentences. “Ajker assignment-ta submit korte hobe” illustrates insertion within a Bangla frame. The structural location differs, but a single inserted item may be difficult to distinguish from an established borrowing without community evidence.

Switches also differ socially. Situational switching accompanies a change in participants or activity, such as choosing English when a non-Bangla-speaking visitor joins. Metaphorical switching changes the social interpretation without a comparable change in setting, perhaps using an official English phrase ironically among friends. Blom and Gumperz help explain this distinction.

Speakers switch for several reasons: inclusion, clarification, quotation, emphasis, topic change, solidarity, identity, or distance. A student may use an English technical label and explain it in Bangla because the course employs that label. The choice need not indicate incompetence or pretension. Gumperz treats switching as a contextualization cue, while Myers-Scotton examines choices against expected social relationships through markedness.

Effects depend on participants. Switching can improve comprehension or strengthen belonging. It can also exclude a listener or reproduce a prestige hierarchy. A useful analysis identifies the context and consequence rather than declaring every switch beneficial or harmful.

Code-switching must be distinguished from language shift. A community can maintain Bangla while regularly using English expressions. Shift requires evidence of replacement across habitual domains or generations, such as declining child acquisition. Translanguaging further directs attention to the integrated resources multilingual speakers use, rather than viewing every communicative activity only as separate code alternations.

Code choices are therefore socially meaningful and often skilled. Their interpretation requires attention to structural form, relationships, purpose, and community norms. In teaching, familiar-language resources can support understanding while planned English tasks develop target-language competence.

S11 Diglossia and H and L varieties

Diglossia concerns a relatively stable social allocation of varieties to different functions. Ferguson's classical account (1959) describes ordinary vernacular varieties alongside a prestigious, superposed variety associated with formal learning and particular institutional uses. H and L indicate relative social status and function rather than linguistic intelligence.

In the classical model, H is associated with formal writing, respected literary traditions, and prepared institutional discourse. L is acquired in childhood and used in everyday conversation. H receives stronger codification and prestige, while L remains central to ordinary social life. The division can persist across generations.

Ferguson describes function, prestige, literary heritage, acquisition, standardization, stability, grammar, lexicon, and phonology. Their relationships help characterize his historical cases. They should not become claims that every L lacks literature or systematic grammar. A prestige hierarchy is a social evaluation.

Arabic provides a useful classical illustration, with a prestigious formal variety and colloquial varieties having differentiated uses. Actual contemporary communication contains overlaps and intermediate forms. Ferguson also examined Swiss German, Greek, and the Haitian Creole/French relationship. His Greek description should not be presented as an unchanged current arrangement.

Fishman extends diglossia to functionally allocated distinct languages. This separates the concept from a rule requiring two varieties to receive the same language name. Bilingualism concerns speakers' resources, while diglossia concerns the social allocation of those resources. Bilingual ability can exist without a stable functional division, and a society can divide functions across groups without every individual commanding both codes.

Ordinary formal and informal style-shifting is therefore insufficient to prove diglossia. An analysis must identify the community, relevant varieties, functions, acquisition, and social organization. A historically situated account of Sadhu and Cholito Bangla may be useful, but present-day English/Bangla use cannot automatically be equated with Ferguson's model.

Diglossia helps explain how language functions and prestige are socially organized. Its value lies in investigating the division and its stability, while recognizing that education, policy, migration, and identity can change the arrangement.

S12 Pidgin and creole development and comparison

Pidgins and creoles are contact-language categories. In the traditional definition, a pidgin initially develops for communication among groups without a shared language and has no community of native speakers. A creole functions as a community language and is often acquired as a first language. Their histories must be explained rather than inferred only from their names.

Early pidgins commonly serve restricted tasks such as trade or labour contact. They often draw vocabulary from a lexifier and develop patterns influenced by the contact situation and contributing languages. Reduced resources relative to those languages do not mean random grammar or an absence of conventions. A pidgin is not simply any learner's imperfect English.

The traditional creolization account describes expansion when a pidgin becomes a community's first language. Speakers need it for a broader range of purposes, including stories, explanations, relationships, and complex social life. Structural and lexical resources develop with those functions. Haitian Creole illustrates a distinct developed contact language with substantial French lexical influence.

The central comparison concerns acquisition and functions. Early pidgins have no native-speaking community and often restricted uses, while creoles commonly have first-language users and broad functions. However, expanded pidgins can be stable and multifunctional. A creole need not be officially standardized to possess systematic grammar.

Holmes and Wilson (2022) discuss Tok Pisin, whose name preserves its contact history although many users acquire it as a first language. Wardhaugh and Fuller (2015) also examine debates over contact-language formation. Not every creole history contains a clearly identifiable earlier pidgin stage, so the traditional development sequence is useful but not universally sufficient.

Contact with a dominant related standard can produce decreolization and a continuum, with basilectal, mesolectal, and acrolectal varieties. This is a possible social development rather than the inevitable destiny of every creole.

Finally, lingua franca describes a communicative function. A pidgin, creole, or established language can serve it. The categories are connected but not synonymous. A good comparison recognizes both the traditional distinction and the diversity of actual contact-language histories.

S13 Language change and its social causes

Language change involves alterations in linguistic forms, meanings, and practices over time. Social factors influence which variants spread or persist, although internal linguistic patterns also matter. Language shift is a distinct process in which a community replaces one habitual language with another.

Contact and migration introduce resources across groups. Borrowed vocabulary may become conventional, and pronunciation patterns can spread through repeated interaction. Urbanization and mobility connect previously separate networks. Technology creates new communication settings and lexical needs, but a new expression is not automatically a permanent change.

Prestige influences adoption. Speakers may acquire a feature because it signals education or opportunity. They may retain another because it marks local loyalty. Overt and covert prestige help explain these different orientations. Labov's Martha's Vineyard study connects pronunciation with local identity, demonstrating that variation can express social positioning.

Networks influence the spread. Close, dense relationships can reinforce local norms, while links across groups can transmit innovations. Milroy's work therefore complements analyses based only on broad class categories. Gender and age can matter through social roles, access, and peer practices; they should not be treated as natural causes operating identically everywhere.

Change from above involves relatively conscious adoption associated with prestige; change from below develops below ordinary conscious awareness. These terms do not simply label upper and lower classes. Apparent-time comparisons can reveal age differences, but real-time evidence helps distinguish continuing change from age grading.

Shift requires further analysis. If schooling and employment increasingly require another language and families stop transmitting their existing language, replacement may occur across generations. Holmes and Wilson's maintenance-and-shift chapter discusses economic, demographic, attitudinal, and institutional factors. Bilingualism does not inevitably lead to abandonment; family practices and institutional support can sustain both languages.

Language change consequently reflects interaction between linguistic resources and social life. A strong answer identifies a specific form or pattern, explains the mechanism promoting it, and evaluates evidence over time. It avoids treating all innovation as decay or every multilingual choice as language shift.

S14 How sociolinguistics helps an EFL teacher

Sociolinguistics helps an EFL teacher understand language as contextual communication rather than grammar alone. It explains how relationships, varieties, identities, and institutions influence what learners understand and produce. Its practical value appears in teaching choices and assessment.

First, the teacher can develop communicative competence. Hymes shows that grammatical knowledge does not establish appropriate use. Role-play can compare asking a friend for notes with requesting help from an unfamiliar lecturer. Learners discuss participants, purposes, tone, and expectations, then adapt wording. This teaches reasons behind language choices.

Second, knowledge of dialects prevents deficit judgements. A learner's home variety may have systematic features that differ from the target standard. The teacher should explain academic conventions while respecting existing competence. Labov's research and Wardhaugh and Fuller's education discussion support distinguishing difference from inability.

Third, the teacher can manage multilingual resources purposefully. A brief familiar-language explanation may clarify a difficult concept, followed by an English task requiring meaningful use. Code-switching is assessed through its learning function rather than automatically condemned. Additional-language learning should expand repertoire instead of making the home language a source of shame.

Fourth, World Englishes encourages attention to variety and intelligibility. Kachru's circles illustrate different historical roles of English, but competence is not restricted to the Inner Circle. Listening tasks can include varied accents. A consistent teaching model remains useful, while successful communication need not require imitation of one prestige accent.

Fifth, sociolinguistics makes academic access explicit. Learners need explanations of genre, register, argument, and assessment conventions. Requiring an unfamiliar style without teaching it can reproduce inequality. Accessible practice helps students acquire valued resources rather than be judged by prior exposure alone.

Finally, sociolinguistic knowledge informs policy. Teachers can report language barriers, adapt materials, and contribute to acquisition planning. Effective English teaching can expand opportunity when it is available broadly rather than only to privileged groups.

An informed EFL teacher therefore combines language accuracy with contextual appropriateness, respect for variation, multilingual support, and fair access. The aim is competent participation across settings, supported by clear teaching and meaningful assessment.

P1 Language policy and the types of planning

Language policy concerns principles, practices, beliefs, and management arrangements governing language use. Language planning involves deliberate efforts to influence functions, structures, learning, and social value. They interact, but a written policy does not automatically generate successful use.

Spolsky distinguishes practices, beliefs, and management. A school may officially respect all languages while teachers value only English and discourage pupils' home-language use. Studying the handbook alone would miss this gap. Policy analysis must therefore examine what people do and believe as well as formal interventions.

Status planning concerns functions, recognition, and rights. Declaring a language authorized for public services changes its institutional position. Corpus planning concerns resources such as spelling, grammar, dictionaries, and terminology. The status/corpus distinction is associated with Kloss and is developed in the supplied textbooks.

Acquisition planning concerns the opportunities through which languages are learned. Cooper gives it particular importance alongside status and corpus. Teacher recruitment, literacy programs, and supported additional-language learning are relevant actions. Prestige planning concerns social evaluation and willingness to use a language, although frameworks differ in whether they distinguish it separately from status.

One project can require all these dimensions. A minority-language school program needs recognized functions, usable materials, prepared teachers, and positive community attitudes. Calling the entire program only corpus planning because it includes books would overlook its other requirements.

Planning can be top-down through authorities or bottom-up through communities. Family language practices and community literacy projects demonstrate that it is not confined to national legislation. Local action can also shape wider policy through advocacy and institutional partnerships.

The purposes include access, literacy, unity, identity, opportunity, and maintenance. These can conflict, and interventions distribute benefits unevenly. Wardhaugh and Fuller (2015) therefore connect planning with social justice and power rather than treating it as neutral technical work.

A strong account distinguishes the types while explaining their coordination. Success depends on whether resources, learning, institutional roles, and social acceptance support one another, and whether the resulting arrangements address the needs of affected speakers.

P2 Aims and challenges of policy in multilingual societies

Language policy in a multilingual society organizes communication while influencing identity, opportunity, and rights. Its aims may include effective administration, wider education, national cohesion, economic participation, and maintenance of minority languages. These aims require negotiation because their costs and benefits are not distributed equally.

A shared language can facilitate communication across groups. Promoting a widely used lingua franca, as illustrated by Swahili in Tanzania, can support national and institutional roles. However, shared communication need not require the elimination of local languages. National and official functions should be distinguished from the languages people use within families and communities.

Educational access is another aim. Pupils need to understand content while developing additional-language competence. A familiar-language foundation with planned multilingual development can support participation. Requiring an unfamiliar medium without support may confuse limited language access with limited academic ability.

Policies may also widen employment and international opportunities through English or another widely used language. The fairness of this aim depends on access to effective learning. If only privileged learners receive adequate teaching, the policy can reproduce elite closure rather than distribute opportunity.

Implementation presents resource challenges. Materials require usable language resources, teachers need preparation, and assessment must match the program. Recognition without staffing, funding, and actual lessons remains largely declarative. Corpus, status, acquisition, and prestige work therefore need coordination.

Political and attitudinal challenges also matter. Selecting one language can advantage its speakers; families may fear that minority-language education reduces mobility; officials may treat multilingual provision as inefficient. Consultation should identify these concerns and consider practical solutions rather than assume uniform acceptance.

Spolsky's practices, beliefs, and management distinguish declared policy from the conditions under which it operates. Tollefson's critical perspective asks whose interests the arrangements protect. Evaluation should examine learning, participation, access, and transmission across groups, rather than count materials alone.

An effective multilingual policy combines workable communication with equitable access and respect for linguistic diversity. Its success depends on the coherence of its goals, resources, practices, and outcomes, not simply the number of languages mentioned in an official statement.

P3 A coordinated language planning proposal for Bangladesh

Language planning in Bangladesh should be analyzed through the roles of Bangla, English, and minority languages. Bangla's status is closely connected with collective identity and access, English supports many international and academic purposes, and minority-language speakers require participation without the unnecessary loss of their existing resources.

Holmes and Wilson (2022, p. 146) discuss the 1952 demonstrations for official recognition of Bengali. The Language Movement illustrates status planning because the issue concerns valued functions and recognition. The historical case also connects language with collective identity and participation. Corpus development is a different, complementary task: it concerns the language resources needed for particular functions.

Corpus planning develops resources. Dictionaries, spelling guidance, terminology, and translations can support wider functions. Holmes and Wilson's Tanzania case (2022, pp. 149–156) shows how selection, codification, elaboration, and acceptance work together. A Bangladesh proposal can apply the same questions: which resources are needed, who will develop them, and how will speakers use them? The proposal should be presented as an application of the model rather than a report of an unexamined program.

Acquisition planning should make additional-language learning effective and widely available. English opportunities require trained teachers, purposeful practice, and usable materials. For pupils whose home language differs from the school medium, planned support can improve access while they develop Bangla and English. Teaching a language as a subject should be distinguished from using it as a medium for other content.

A proposed school-level program would begin with language-use mapping and family consultation. It would establish the functions of each resource, prepare locally reviewed materials, train teachers, and pilot activities before wider adoption. Prestige-related work would challenge the assumption that a home language is unsuitable for learning.

Evaluation would distinguish inputs and outputs from outcomes. The school would observe participation, assess comprehension and literacy, gather family feedback, and examine whether opportunities reach different groups. Printed books or attendance alone would not establish successful acquisition or continuing transmission. Revision would follow the evidence.

Spolsky's framework helps examine consistency between practices, beliefs, and management; the planning types explain what action is required. The proposed approach combines international opportunity with access and linguistic respect. It is a reasoned application of theory, not a claim that the entire proposal is already national policy.

Register

Holmes and Wilson (2022, p. 687) describe register broadly as language used in particular situations and more narrowly as specialist language associated with activities or groups. Medical communication may use words such as “dyspnoea,” while an explanation to a patient may use “shortness of breath.” The activity remains medical, but audience and purpose affect the wording. Register differs from dialect, which is associated with a speaker's region or social group, and overlaps with style, which concerns contextual choices such as formality. Wardhaugh and Fuller (2015, p. 53) connect registers with occupations and social activities. A good short note supplies a definition, an example, and this distinction.

Standard and nonstandard language

A standard variety has relatively established norms supported by institutions such as schools, administration, and publishing. A nonstandard variety differs from those norms but possesses systematic grammar. Institutional prestige does not establish superior intelligence or expressive capacity. Standard English can be spoken with different accents, so it should not be equated with RP. Standards can support common conventions, while standard-language ideology may stigmatize regional forms. A fair educational approach teaches access to academic conventions without condemning a learner's home variety. Wardhaugh and Fuller (2015), Chapter 2, explain the standard–dialect hierarchy and the distinction between linguistic structure and social value.

Definition reference. Holmes and Wilson 2022, p. 690.

Linguistic determinism

Linguistic determinism is the strong claim that language determines or confines thought. It is associated with debates conventionally labelled the Sapir–Whorf hypothesis. Linguistic relativity makes the more qualified claim that linguistic categories can influence habitual attention or task performance. Bangla kinship terms can make some relationships readily expressible, but an English speaker can understand those relationships through explanation. Missing a single word does not establish conceptual impossibility. Translation, bilingualism, and nonverbal reasoning challenge strong determinism. Evidence from spatial and colour tasks requires careful interpretation rather than universal conclusions. Holmes and Wilson (2022), Chapter 13, discuss the language–cognition relationship and its limits.

Definition reference. Holmes and Wilson 2022, p. 680.

Positive face and negative face

In Brown and Levinson's politeness model, positive face concerns the desire for approval and acceptance; negative face concerns freedom from unwanted interference. The labels do not mean good and bad personalities. Calling a student's argument foolish can threaten positive face, while demanding their laptop for a week can threaten negative face. One act can threaten both. Positive-politeness strategies may emphasize shared purpose; negative-politeness strategies recognize the hearer's freedom and the request's burden. Their appropriateness depends on distance, power, imposition, and community norms. Goffman's account of face underlies the discussion. Wardhaugh and Fuller (2015), Chapter 10, connect these concepts with interaction.

Definition reference. Wardhaugh and Fuller 2015, p. 414.

Gender bias and sexist language

Holmes and Wilson (2022, p. 453) explain that sexist language encodes stereotyped attitudes to women and men. Examples include generic masculine references, unequal occupational labels, and pairs of words with different connotations. “Every student should bring his book” uses a masculine pronoun for an intended general reference, whereas “Students should bring their books” expresses that reference differently. Master and mistress illustrate that paired forms can differ in meaning as well as gender. Wardhaugh and Fuller (2015, pp. 314–318) examine these asymmetries and the relationship between language change and attitudes. Explain the particular form, its interpretation, and its context; a list of supposedly biased words alone is not an analysis.

Definition reference. Holmes and Wilson 2022, p. 688.

Age and language style

Age influences language through peer groups, life-stage expectations, historical exposure, and social roles. Young speakers may use expressions associated with their peers, while professional settings encourage other choices. Age grading occurs when a feature belongs to a stage of life and recurs as new people enter that stage. Generational change occurs when a cohort's innovation contributes to longer-term change. A slang term used by adolescents therefore does not automatically predict the future language. Apparent-time comparisons require caution and may need real-time evidence. Individual speakers also adapt to audience and purpose. Holmes and Wilson (2022), Chapters 7 and 9, explain these distinctions.

Definition reference. Holmes and Wilson 2022, p. 667.

Lingua franca

A lingua franca is a shared medium among speakers whose first languages differ. English at an international academic conference can serve this function, as can Swahili across linguistic groups in East Africa. The label identifies a role in communication rather than a special structural category. A lingua franca may be a participant's first language or an additional language for everyone present. It can be an established language, a pidgin, or a creole. Widespread use can increase its social importance but can also place pressure on other languages. Holmes and Wilson (2022), Chapter 4, discuss lingua francas alongside other varieties and multilingual contexts.

Definition reference. Holmes and Wilson 2022, p. 680.

Creole

A creole is a developed contact language functioning as a community language and often acquired as a first language. The traditional account describes creolization as a pidgin acquiring native speakers and expanding its resources and functions. Haitian Creole is a distinct language with substantial French lexical influence. Creoles have systematic grammar and can serve complex social purposes whether or not they possess an official standard. Not every history contains an identifiable pidgin stage, and an expanded pidgin can also have broad functions. The distinction should therefore consider acquisition and history rather than a language's name alone. Wardhaugh and Fuller (2015), Chapter 5, examine these issues.

Definition reference. Holmes and Wilson 2022, p. 671.

Code switching

Code-switching is alternation between languages or varieties within interaction. It may occur across or within sentences. “Ami ekhon jabo. I will call you later” illustrates a switch across sentences. Situational switching accompanies a change in participants or activity; metaphorical switching changes the social interpretation without an equivalent new situation. Speakers may switch for quotation, inclusion, emphasis, clarification, solidarity, or distance. Gumperz treats switching as a contextualization cue. Its effects depend on the audience: it may clarify for one listener and exclude another. It does not by itself prove language shift, which requires replacement across habitual domains or generations. Definitions of switching and mixing overlap in scholarship.

Definition reference. Holmes and Wilson 2022, p. 670.

Sociolect

A sociolect is a variety associated with a social group, commonly investigated in relation to class or other social affiliations. Its features can include pronunciation, vocabulary, and grammar. A sociolect is not simply professional jargon, which relates more directly to register, and it need not form a completely separate language system. Labov's variation research shows how particular features distribute systematically across social positions and speaking situations. A feature may carry overt prestige in institutions or covert prestige within a group. Speakers can adjust their use, so social association should not become a fixed identity test. Wardhaugh and Fuller (2015), Chapters 2 and 6, discuss social dialects and variation.

Definition reference. Holmes and Wilson 2022, p. 689.

Diglossia

Diglossia concerns relatively stable allocation of varieties to different social functions. Ferguson's classical model distinguishes prestigious learned H uses from everyday vernacular L uses, with differences in acquisition, codification, and literary traditions. H and L indicate social position rather than inherent linguistic quality. Arabic supplies a classical illustration, although actual present-day practice is more varied. Fishman extends the concept to differentiated functions across distinct languages. Bilingualism concerns a person's repertoire; diglossia concerns society's functional organization. A formal and an informal English conversation alone do not establish diglossia. Specify the community, varieties, period, and allocation. Ferguson (1959) is the central reference for the classical account.

Definition reference. Holmes and Wilson 2022, p. 672.

Education and language choice

Education can expand access to standard forms, academic registers, and additional languages. It can also make one variety the condition of success and devalue another. A student using a local dialect at home and academic language in class demonstrates a varied repertoire. Their home speech is not evidence of low intelligence. Teachers should explain valued conventions and support learners whose familiar language differs from the instructional medium. English taught as a subject differs from English used to teach science. Education therefore affects both individual choices and language status through acquisition and institutional policy. Wardhaugh and Fuller (2015), Chapter 13, connect these issues with inequality and multilingual education.

Speech acts

A speech act is an action performed through language, such as requesting, promising, apologizing, or declaring. Austin distinguishes locutionary content, illocutionary action, and perlocutionary effect. “Please submit the script” has linguistic content, performs a directive, and may cause submission. The effect is not guaranteed. An indirect act uses one conventional form to perform another action, as when “Could you close the window?” functions as a request. Holmes and Wilson (2022, p. 390) discuss promises and threats as commissives. Felicity conditions include relevant authority and circumstances: an unauthorized spectator cannot officially declare an examination cancelled. Wardhaugh and Fuller (2015), Chapter 10, introduce these distinctions.

Face and politeness in communication

Face concerns the socially recognized image a person seeks to maintain in interaction. Goffman's work is foundational, and Brown and Levinson distinguish approval-oriented positive face from autonomy-oriented negative face. Politeness can manage threats arising in criticism, requests, refusals, or disagreement. Saying “I see why you chose that example; perhaps another would clarify the point” can mitigate criticism while still evaluating it. Directness is not always impolite, and indirectness is not always helpful. Social distance, relative power, imposition, and local conventions affect interpretation. A good account explains what a strategy does in context rather than listing courteous words. Cultural variation limits universal claims about the most polite form.

AAVE

African American Vernacular English refers to varieties associated with many African American communities. Speakers differ, and the label does not imply that every person uses all the same features. Its grammar is systematic. Invariant habitual be, as in “She be working late,” can describe a recurring activity, while “She is working late” may describe the present occasion. Treating the first as an unsuccessful version of the second loses meaning. Social stigma reflects evaluation and power rather than absence of grammar. Labov's work challenges deficit accounts of nonstandard speech. Wardhaugh and Fuller (2015), Chapters 2 and 13, discuss features, history, and educational consequences.

Linguistic variable

A linguistic variable is a feature with alternative forms, called variants, whose distribution can be studied. The ng and n pronunciations in an ending such as “walking” can be investigated as variants of (ING). Pronunciation of postvocalic r is another well-known variable in Labov's research. The linguistic variable should be distinguished from the social factors used to explain its distribution: class, style, or age are not themselves pronunciation variants. Comparing frequencies can reveal systematic relationships, but correlation requires interpretation and does not prove a single cause. Wardhaugh and Fuller (2015), Chapter 6, explain variables, variants, sampling, and quantitative variation studies.

Definition reference. Holmes and Wilson 2022, p. 681.

Formal and informal language

Formal language commonly involves planned wording, explicit connections, and institutional conventions. Informal language often relies on shared knowledge, familiarity, and spontaneous interaction. Their usefulness depends on the purpose. An academic explanation may require explicit reasoning, while an intimate family exchange can communicate efficiently through implied context. Formality should not be confused with correctness, politeness, or a particular language: both Bangla and English allow varied styles. Nor is register identical to formality, since a professional activity can contain both careful and familiar exchanges. Holmes and Wilson (2022), Chapter 10, explain how audience, status, solidarity, and setting shape style.

Language as a tool of domination

Language can contribute to domination when institutions privilege one code or variety and restrict access through it. A requirement for costly English qualifications may reproduce inequality when the actual task does not need them and effective teaching is unavailable to many applicants. Bourdieu explains the unequal value of resources through linguistic markets; Myers-Scotton's elite closure identifies language arrangements protecting privilege. Ideological labels can also make exclusion appear natural, as when a regional accent is treated as proof of low ability. The mechanism must be demonstrated in context: a justified language requirement is not automatically domination. Wardhaugh and Fuller (2015) connect language, education, policy, and social justice.

Ethnolect

An ethnolect is a variety associated with an ethnic community through social history, contact, networks, and identity. African American English and Chicano English are widely discussed examples. The association does not mean every community member speaks identically or that outsiders cannot learn features. Language is not biologically inherited with ethnicity. An ethnolect may help signal solidarity and belonging, while listeners may also stereotype its users. It can overlap with regional and class patterns. A sound analysis identifies linguistic features and social meanings without treating them as reliable ancestry tests. Wardhaugh and Fuller (2015), Chapter 2, and Holmes and Wilson (2022), Chapter 8, provide relevant discussion.

Definition reference. Wardhaugh and Fuller 2015, p. 404.

Pidgin

In the traditional definition, a pidgin is a contact language initially used among groups without a shared language and without native speakers. Early uses often involve trade or labour, with resources reduced relative to contributing languages. The forms nevertheless develop conventions; pidgin does not mean random errors. A lexifier supplies much vocabulary, while other languages and social conditions contribute to development. Some pidgins expand and stabilize before widespread native acquisition. A creole commonly has community first-language use and broader functions, although origins vary. A pidgin can also serve as a lingua franca. Holmes and Wilson (2022), Chapter 4, explain these linked categories.

Definition reference. Holmes and Wilson 2022, p. 685.

Bilingualism

Holmes and Wilson (2022, p. 668) define bilingualism as “Knowledge of two languages.” A bilingual speaker's abilities and use may differ across domains and tasks. A student can use Bangla confidently in family conversation and English more frequently for academic reading. Individual bilingualism concerns the person's repertoire, whereas societal bilingualism concerns the distribution of languages in a community. It differs from diglossia, which concerns the social allocation of functions. The books' discussion of domains helps explain why unequal proficiency does not make the label meaningless. Relevant reading is Holmes and Wilson (2022, pp. 23–38) and Wardhaugh and Fuller (2015, pp. 83–95).

Language shift

Language shift occurs when a community gradually replaces one habitual language with another across domains or generations. It differs from individual attrition, which concerns declining proficiency, and from changes within a language. A hypothetical immigrant family may retain its heritage language among grandparents while children increasingly use only the surrounding majority language. The pattern is not inevitable. Employment, schooling, networks, attitudes, and family transmission influence it. English words inserted into Bangla do not by themselves establish shift. Holmes and Wilson (2022), Chapter 3, explain both pressures and maintenance measures. Fishman's work emphasizes transmission when considering how shift may be reversed.

Definition reference. Holmes and Wilson 2022, p. 678.

Language policy and language planning

Language policy concerns the principles, practices, beliefs, and management arrangements governing use. Language planning involves deliberate attempts to influence functions, resources, acquisition, or social value. A school may declare support for multilingual learning, then plan staffing, materials, and lessons to pursue it. The two interact, but written policy does not guarantee implementation. Spolsky distinguishes practices, beliefs, and management, helping identify gaps between declarations and everyday conditions. Planning can occur through governments, institutions, communities, or families. Wardhaugh and Fuller (2015), Chapter 14, explain why LPP must be understood in relation to social interests and inequality.

Status planning and corpus planning

Status planning concerns the functions, recognition, and rights associated with a language. Authorizing a language for a public service changes its institutional role. Corpus planning concerns developed linguistic resources, such as orthography, dictionaries, grammar, and terminology. Writing a dictionary therefore differs from giving the language an official function, although one project may require both. The distinction is associated with Kloss. Recognition without usable resources can remain ineffective, while resources without supported functions may see little use. Acquisition and prestige work are also often needed. Holmes and Wilson (2022), Chapter 5, explain the interaction between social decisions and linguistic development.

Definition reference. Wardhaugh and Fuller 2015, p. 418.

Acquisition planning

Acquisition planning concerns who learns a language, through what opportunities, and with what support. Cooper gives it a prominent role alongside status and corpus planning. Teacher recruitment, literacy programs, adult learning, immersion, and access to English instruction can be examples. Books alone do not constitute an effective learning system: prepared teachers, meaningful practice, and assessment matter. A school can teach English as a subject without making it the medium of every lesson. Evaluation should examine actual learning and equitable access, not only enrolment. Holmes and Wilson (2022), Chapter 5, discuss acquisition planning and its relationship with wider language goals.

Prestige planning

Prestige planning concerns how a language or variety is valued and whether people are willing to use it. Campaigns challenging stigma, respected public use, and positive representation may support acceptance. A school could recognize pupils' home-language knowledge instead of treating it as embarrassing. Prestige work differs from developing spelling rules and cannot substitute for teachers or materials. Frameworks vary: some distinguish prestige separately, while others connect it with status planning. The essential issue is how attitudes affect use and implementation. Holmes and Wilson (2022), Chapter 5, connect acceptance, status, and prestige in the development of national and official languages.

Haugen and standardization

Haugen's account connects selection, codification, elaboration, and acceptance. Selection chooses the norm; codification establishes conventions; elaboration develops resources for wider functions; acceptance concerns recognition and use. These interact rather than form an inflexible one-time sequence. A later formulation gives explicit attention to implementation through institutions and education. Codification is therefore not synonymous with the entire process, and distributing a norm does not guarantee acceptance. A useful examination diagram places social selection and acceptance alongside linguistic codification and elaboration. Holmes and Wilson (2022), Chapter 5, reproduce the four-component matrix and apply planning through case studies.

National and official languages

A national language commonly symbolizes collective identity; an official language is authorized for specified institutional or governmental uses. Their precise roles depend on the particular legal and social setting. A language may serve both purposes, and one country can have more than one relevant language. Holmes and Wilson (2022, p. 146) describe the 1952 demand for official recognition of Bengali. This case illustrates the social importance of a language's formal functions. National, official, home, and instructional roles should be distinguished. Holmes and Wilson (2022), Chapter 5, explain how political power, existing communication, and identity influence the selection of national and official languages.

Definition reference. Holmes and Wilson 2022, p. 683.

Mother tongue based multilingual education

Mother tongue based multilingual education describes an educational arrangement that uses familiar home-language resources while developing additional languages. For an examination answer, explain the actual medium, learning goals, and role of each language. Wardhaugh and Fuller (2015, pp. 351–355) discuss instrumental use of a home language, accommodation, and multilingual programs. These arrangements differ in whether the home language is temporary support or a sustained educational resource. Teacher preparation, materials, assessment, and learning opportunities determine how the plan operates. A hypothetical school example should connect status, corpus, and acquisition work rather than assume that distributing books establishes learning.

Ethnolinguistic vitality

Ethnolinguistic vitality concerns conditions supporting a group's linguistic continuity and collective distinctiveness. Giles, Bourhis, and Taylor identify status, demography, and institutional support. These include social value, speaker distribution, and support through education, media, or public institutions. A small community may maintain strong transmission, while a large group may face declining child acquisition. Numbers alone therefore cannot predict survival. Positive attitudes need practical opportunities for ordinary use. The framework complements attention to family transmission and wider endangerment indicators. Holmes and Wilson (2022), Chapter 3, discuss vitality-related conditions when explaining maintenance and shift.

Definition reference. Holmes and Wilson 2022, p. 673.

Language policy and access

Wardhaugh and Fuller (2015, p. 368) relate status planning to the functions of a language and the rights of its users. Recognition can permit a language to be used in education, administration, or other institutional activities. Actual access also requires staff, resources, and working arrangements. For example, recognizing a language for a service does not establish that someone can receive that service when no trained staff are available. A planning answer should distinguish the stated rule from its operation and examine the consequences for the relevant speakers. This connects the concept with status planning and implementation.
